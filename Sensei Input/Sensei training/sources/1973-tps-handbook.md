## Preface: Practice Rather than Theory (論より実践)
**Author: Taiichi Ohno**

> "For someone who is striving for rationalization, everything becomes about implementation, or a challenge about how close you can get to it."

Ohno defines JIT for the workplace not as an abstract scheduling concept but as a concrete instruction:

> "Go get what you need, in the amount you need, when you need it."

The fundamental idea of TPS is that the following process goes to pick up from the preceding process -- reversing the conventional push logic.

The trick to TPS has three parts:

1. Eliminating unnecessary activities -- and doing it thoroughly.
2. Developing eyes that can find the unnecessary activity so that you can eliminate it.
3. Thinking of the method to eliminate the unnecessary activities you have found.

This is not a one-time effort. Ohno insists on never waning or letting up, no matter how long it takes, no matter how far you must go.

He called balancing quality, quantity, and cost the "workplace technique" or "manufacturing technique" or **MIE** (Moukeru IE / profit-making industrial engineering).

## Chapter 1: Cost & Labor Reduction Method

### Section 1 -- Toyota Cost & Labor Reduction Method

**Basic idea of TPS:** A sequence of operations improving productivity by reducing unnecessary, unreasonable, and uneven activities, thereby decreasing production costs. Based on IE theory but developed exclusively for Toyota over a long period.

**Key concepts introduced:**
- Leveling Production Plans
- Minimize Lot Size
- Just-In-Time
- Scientific Attitude

**Scientific attitude:** The actual situation is the starting point. Investigate root cause. Develop countermeasure. Facts at the workplace are more accurate than data or reports.

**5-Whys method:** Ask "Why, why, why..." until root cause is found.

**Making problems visible:**

> "If the problem is known, it is relatively easy to implement countermeasures. The hard part is finding out what the real problem is."

The objective of problem-solving should be clear: root cause must be identified, not just provisional measures.

**Take Countermeasures for Every Single Failure** -- even if a failure occurs only once in 1,000.

**Be Practical:** Step-by-step measures; attach importance to results.

**Sequence of improvement (start from work, end at design):**
1. People improvement (training)
2. Process improvement (including layout)
3. Equipment improvement
4. Design improvement

**Man-hours vs. Number of People:** 0.9 person saving doesn't reduce a person. The distinction between labor saving and people saving is critical.

**Automation vs. Autonomation:**
- Automation = machine runs by itself but needs a person to stop it.
- Autonomation = machine detects problems and stops automatically.

**Checking is Reflection:** Improvement is finished only when intended results are obtained.

**Economic Efficiency is the Criteria:**

> "Whether this is economically beneficial or not?"

- Operation ratio should be decided by required production amount, not sunk costs.
- Spare capacity allows you to practice improvement.
- The workplace is a single organism, not just hands following brains.
- Emphasize the capacity of adapting to change.

---

### Section 2 -- Labor Reduction and Cost

The objective of labor reduction is cost reduction. All evaluation criteria are based on whether they can achieve cost reductions.

**Deciding between A and B:** Decide based on what is more beneficial for Toyota as a whole.

> "We do not do just anything because it is profitable. Instead we must go for the plan that we believed to be the most profitable among all the other profitable options."

**Unnecessary activity defined:** Factors in the production workplace which only increase cost. Too many people, excessive equipment, too much inventory. Primary unnecessary activity causes secondary unnecessary activity.

**The overstock cascade** (the largest unnecessary activity):

Excess inventory leads to needing a warehouse, which needs people, which needs forklifts, which needs management, which leads to rust and damage, which needs repair, which needs computers, which creates a perceived capacity shortage, which triggers capital investment, which creates even more inventory.

**Sunk costs:** Costs already paid are irrelevant to future plans.

> "Is it really a loss to not make expensive equipment operate fully?"

No. Choose by operating cost, not purchase price.

**Reducing people reduces costs:** Index takt time from required number. One person operating multiple machines.

> "It is wrong to think of leaving a machine idle as a loss."

**Continuous costs vs. temporary costs:** Daily small costs are easy to overlook; temporary big costs are noticed. Convert daily costs to annual to see real magnitude.

**How to use ratios:** Utilization rate decline is not automatically a loss. Producing only the necessary amount when needed is most profitable. Running at 100% capacity creates mountains of inventory with no income change.

### Section 3 -- Labor Reduction and Quality

**Quality is built into the production process:** Every single person is responsible. The following process is the customer. 100% inspection is built into the process, not performed by separate inspectors.

**If a defect is found in the following process:**
1. Immediately contact preceding process
2. Stop production
3. Investigate root cause
4. Implement countermeasures

**Quality is the true value of improvement.** Quality is the first priority, period. Safety in automobiles is a social responsibility.

**Standard work and quality:** If defects persist, either standard work is not being followed, or there is a defect in machinery, equipment, or tools. The critical question: understand WHY people are not following standard work.

**Takt time and quality:**

> "Takt time and the number of people are unrelated."

If a worker cannot finish in takt time, the line should stop for 10 seconds every cycle rather than have the worker cut corners.

**The goal of the inspector:** Not just pass/fail, but responsible for workplace analysis of why failure occurred, determine cause, stop reoccurrence.

> "The inspector must be a teacher."

> "Not to toss out the defective product but to reduce them to zero."

**Poka-Yoke (error-proofing):** Six categories of mechanisms (A-F) covering:
- Operational errors
- Product problems
- Incomplete work
- Preceding process defects

Additional error-proofing methods:
- Signs (visual)
- Jigs (won't accept wrong products)
- Autonomation (stop on problem)

### Section 4 -- Labor Reduction and Safety

> "There is no labor reduction where safety is not considered."

**Relationship between labor reduction and safety:** Seemingly conflicting values are actually consistent. Eliminating unnecessary, uneven, and unreasonable activities improves both productivity and safety.

Accidents are caused by behaviors that are unnecessary, uneven, or unreasonable. The Japanese character for "Injury" contains the meaning "This is odd if I do say so myself."

**4-S (the original, before 5S/6S):**
1. Seiri (Sort)
2. Seiton (Organize)
3. Seisou (Clean)
4. Seiketsu (Standardize)

Later consultants added others, "complicating the original program until the tool becomes an independent program, without understanding why it was useful in the first place."

**Safety data (1970):** 878 accidents. Heinrich's Law referenced. 72% caused by unsafe behavior.

**Visual management for leaders:** Immediately recognize unsafe, unstable actions.

**Automation vs. autonomation in safety context:**
- Automation designed for labor-saving (not people-saving) still needs a guard.
- Autonomation with safety switch stops on abnormality -- important for both safety and labor reduction.

**Conveyor example:** A sign says "Turn off switch before climbing onto conveyor." But when the conveyor runs at 80%, maintenance says it can wait, and production pressure exists -- will the worker really follow the rule? The blame goes to the person, but the real reason is incomplete automation. This is a system failure, not a behavior failure.

### Section 5 -- Labor Reduction and Human Relations

**Respect for people as a foundation of TPS:**

> "Labor reduction aims mainly the elimination of unnecessary activity, and it does not mean labor intensification or speeding up."

> "There is nothing more demoralizing for people who are offering their precious energy and time to the company and are forced to waste it. Concentrating their efforts into work that is useful is the beginning of respect for people."

**Coach Omatsu story:** Mutual trust built from thinking from the player's viewpoint, not flattery.

**Supervisor responsibilities:**
- Must have respect for people as foundation
- Put themselves in people's shoes
- Train while fully understanding them
- Proactively confront all problems

**If problems aren't responded to promptly:**

> "People gradually cease to complain."

This is the source of "people problems."

**Assembly line improvement case study (September-December 1946):**
- Takt increased from 16.6 to 18.2 vehicles/hour
- 120-150 min line stops per shift
- Went to production floor, applied ~100 improvements centered on worker difficulties
- Line stops dropped to 20-30 min
- Complaints about "labor intensification" completely disappeared
- 80% of improvements came from the people themselves

Mutual trust starts with participation in improvement activities.

### Section 6 -- How to Promote Labor Reduction Activities

**Work classification:**
- **(A) Unnecessary activity** -- eliminate immediately
- **(B) Non-value-added work** -- must be done under current conditions but is waste
- **(C) Value-adding work**

The proportion of value-adding work is "surprisingly low."

**Overproduction is the worst unnecessary activity.** It hides other waste.

> "The first step to promote labor reduction activities is to eliminate the unnecessary activity of overproduction."

**Takt time formula:**

```
Takt Time = Operating time available per day / Required number of products per day
```

Common error: calculating from equipment capacity or available labor instead of from demand.

**Redistribution of work (critical insight):** Do NOT spread surplus time evenly across workers.

- Bad example: 4 workers each at 0.85 capacity. Waste is invisible.
- Correct approach: Fill 3 workers to 1.0 and leave person 4 at 0.4 (visible surplus), then eliminate the 0.4 through improvement.

Equal redistribution hides the waste and creates psychological resistance to future improvement.

**Sequence of improvement:**
1. **(A)** Immediately eliminate unnecessary activity
2. **(B)** Reduce non-value-added work starting from easiest
3. **(C)** Leave value-adding work as-is

**Work improvement before equipment improvement -- always.** Three reasons:
1. Equipment costs money and might not be needed
2. Equipment improvement is irreversible
3. Equipment in a workplace without good work improvement will likely fail

**Make the problems visible:** A well-organized and standardized workplace.

**Do not be afraid of stopping the line:**

> "Instead of fearing line stops, you should consider them as the springboard for further improvement."

> "A line which does not stop is either perfectly good or extremely bad."

**When reducing people: reduce the best personnel first.** Removing poor performers leads to morale decline. Removing best performers gets active cooperation from others -- they get reassigned to improvement work.

### Section 7 -- Labor Reduction and Supervisors

**Supervisor's circle:**
1. Is line organized and kept tidy? Standard work determined?
2. Make process visually manageable
3. Determine production takt based on needed amount
4. Any wasteful overproduction?
5. Find the waste, eliminate it
6. Keep working patiently and repeat

**Management of abnormalities:** Standardize everything, then focus on deviations.

> "If they cannot recognize what is abnormal, they are not suitable to be a supervisor."

**Examples of abnormalities:**
- Operator A has too little work (waiting, doing non-standard tasks)
- Operator B has too much work (cutting corners, producing defects)
- Operator C helping a machine that should be automatic

**Stopping the line:**

> "If you find the abnormality and clearly understand what the problem is, 50% of the problem is solved."

Choose least expensive, most effective solution. Making the abnormalities visible and noticeable is the essential principle.

> "A supervisor who cannot say 'stop the line,' as well as a supervisor whose line has stopped 2 or 3 times for the same reason both get an 'F.'"

**Implementing the improvement:** Try and see. Work improvement before equipment improvement. Pushing it onto them saying "you should be able to do this" will provoke opposition. Standardize the improvement as a new work routine.

**Supervisor's improvement circle:**

Standardization --> finding abnormality --> investigation of the cause --> improvement --> standardization

**Things to keep in mind:**
1. Observe the workplace constantly
2. Train the team -- "a good human relationship is not built by just earning the favor by being modest and reserved"
3. Look from a broad perspective

> "The best supervisor is one who can do the standardization and promote improvement so that the line can work at its best performance even without them. The ideal supervisor can say: 'Please remove myself from the line.'"

**Supervisors working on the line:**

> "It is not good if supervisor is participating in line work all the time; but at the same time, it is not that they do not need to do so at all."

They must know the difficulty and procedures of the work to improve it.

## Chapter 2: Efficiency

### Section 1 -- What is Efficiency?

**Effectiveness vs. efficiency:**
- Effectiveness = ratio of useful work to total effort (capped at 100%)
- Efficiency = comparison to a standard (can exceed 100%)

**Objective is cost reduction:**

> "If increasing the efficiency became the objective, there is a risk of making management mistakes."

**Operation ratio vs. operational availability:**
- Operation ratio = actual production vs. capacity
- Operational availability = readiness to operate when wanted
- Ideal operational availability is 100%
- Ideal operation ratio is NOT 100%

> "If you drive the car around from morning to night without any purpose, this will only lead to increasing your gas and oil consumption."

**True efficiency vs. apparent (calculated) efficiency:**

10 people making 100 items, improved to make 120. If demand is still 100, making 120 is "apparent increase" -- costs go up. True efficiency: 8 people making 100 items.

> "Production volume = required number" is a major principle.

Two ways to increase efficiency: increase output or reduce workers. Most supervisors choose increasing output (easier). Toyota chooses reducing workers (harder, but the only method that reduces cost when demand is flat or falling).

**Raising efficiency when production quantities decrease** -- this is what separates great supervisors.

> "Few people in the world can raise productivity when production quantities decrease."

---

### Section 2 -- Individual Efficiency and Overall Efficiency

**Boat race analogy:** 8 rowers synchronized at 40-50 strokes/min. One rower going faster upsets the boat. Individual efficiency does not equal overall efficiency.

**Efficiency at three levels:**
1. Individual worker
2. Production line
3. Whole factory

**Work vs. activity (Ugoki vs. Hataraki):**

> "In Toyota, it could be said that we were more active than the Westerners. And yet, we were behind when it came to labor productivity."

More activity does not equal more work.

**Labor density:** Work labor divided by movement labor. Higher ratio = more of person's energy going to important work.

**Automation vs. autonomation (detailed):**

The kanji 自働化 (autonomation) includes the human radical 亻, while 自動化 (automation) has no human radical.

> "The character for 'self 自' refers to the person, and it means allowing the person themselves to stop the conveyor if they ever think 'this is not right!' or 'this is a defective product!'"

**Balance between processes:** The bottleneck determines capability. Help bottleneck processes rather than having fast workers pile up WIP in front of them.

**Cooperation (relay race analogy):** Track relay preferred over swimming relay -- the baton pass zone allows compensation for variation.

> "The work done in the actual workplace must be like a track relay."

**Production leveling:** Reduce variance of types and quantities. Larger lots in upstream processes create more unnecessary activity. Make lots as small as practical.

---

### Section 3 -- Increasing Efficiency by Eliminating Unnecessary Activity

**Fundamental equation:**

```
Current Capacity = Useful Work + Unnecessary Activity
```

**Four types of unnecessary activities (the original categories, before "7 wastes"):**
1. Overproduction
2. Waiting time
3. Transport
4. Processing itself

> "These four categories were further expanded and subdivided into what is commonly referred to today as '7 Wastes'. The objective of defining them was to make it easier for people to see where improvements needed to be made."

**Key method:** All four types should be changed into visible waiting time -- the easiest form of waste to spot and countermeasure.

**Methods for making waste visible:**
- **(A)** Make operators follow standard work thoroughly
- **(B)** Restrict overproduction using kanban
- **(C)** Specify workspace on conveyor

## Chapter 3: Standard Work

### Section 1 -- Standard Work

> "Standard work is alive, it is always incomplete and always has tasks that can be improved."

**Three elements of standard work:**
1. Cycle time
2. Work sequence
3. Standard work-in-process (WIP)

If any one is missing, standard work is not complete.

**Standard work vs. work standards:**
- Work standards = all standards required to carry out standard work (temperatures, tolerances, coolant types)
- Standard work = the combination of people, machines, materials

> "The standard is something the supervisor establishes themselves."

Not developed by IE technicians, but by the leader on the floor.

> "A supervisor at a workplace where the standard work has not been revised regularly, has proved themselves to be an 'incapable leader.'"

> "If there is no standard, there can be no improvement." -- Taiichi Ohno

---

### Section 2 -- Creating Standard Operating Procedures

**Part-specific capacity chart:** Records process order, machine number, basic time (manual labor time, automatic machine time, completion time), cutting tools, processing capacity.

**Standard work combination chart:** A visual tool showing work sequence, work details, and time relationships between:
- Manual work (solid line)
- Automatic feed (dotted line)
- Walking (wavy line)

Includes 11 detailed steps for filling the combination chart.

**Standard work chart (work instruction manual):** A3-size paper showing machine placement, work sequence, standard WIP, cycle time, safety and quality check points.

> "By displaying the standard work chart, the guidance of the supervisor, 'Work should be done like this', becomes visual."

---

### Sections 3-4 -- Work Instructions and Work Instruction Manual

**Job breakdown sheet:** Sets work procedures based on scientific thinking for accurate, quick, easy, safe work.

Three components:
1. Important steps
2. Key points (success/failure, safety, making work easier)
3. Reasons why

Sketches should include quantitative detail. Avoid abstract words.

---

### Section 5 -- Quality Check Table

Incorporated in work instructions for safety parts and critical quality parts. Records:
- What
- Where
- Who
- When
- How
- Why (for quality checks)

---

### Section 6 -- Procedure of Work Based on Standard Work

The supervisor must master standard work first, then teach it. Explain reasons why standard procedures must be followed:

> "Teaching what might happen if it is not followed."

> "Standard work is the center of continuous improvement. Standard work is not something about which you can say 'Now we are good enough' after you establish it."

Use TWI (Training Within Industry) Job Instruction method for teaching.

## Chapter 4: Kanban System

### Section 1 -- About Kanban

Born from the Supermarket System concept. Officially used since August 1963 at Honsha Plant.

**Supermarket principle:** Customer gets what is needed, at the time needed, in the amount needed.

> "Cost reduction routine means, unnecessary activity elimination routine."

**Three types of unnecessary activity (the 3M's):**
- **Muda** (waste)
- **Mura** (unevenness)
- **Muri** (overburden)

**Finding unnecessary activity:** The first step is to clearly define standard work and ensure it is followed. Without standard work, "it would be impossible for everyone to easily spot unnecessary activities."

Kanban is an important visual management tool -- not merely inventory control or production instructions.

---

### Section 2 -- KANBAN Thinking

**Thorough observation of workplace:** Three problems with observation:
1. Bias from experience
2. Superficial observation
3. Insufficient time observing

> "Stay there and keep observing for about half a day."

> "Observe without any bias, as if you were a blank piece of paper."

**Pursuit of rationalization and respect for people:** Compatible, not contradictory. Visual management lets everyone participate in finding waste, which gives even monotonous workers a sense of accomplishment.

**Continuous improvement (Kaizen):** Built on "thorough observation" and "pursuit of rationalization" as its foundation.

**5W1H:** Ask "Why?" 5 times to find root cause.

5-Why example:
1. Machine stops --> why? --> switch broken
2. Switch broken --> why? --> oil in switch case
3. Oil in switch case --> why? --> leaking lubricant pipe joint
4. Leaking pipe joint --> why? --> pipe runs through switch case
5. Pipe runs through switch case --> why? --> (why does it?)

> "5W = 1H" (Five Why's equal One How)

**Drafting improvement proposals:** Consider relationships between all parties.

> "A thinking process based on a series of ideas, avoiding being entrapped by a single way of thinking."

**Implementation is the hardest part:**

> "Success and failure in improvement activities are decided by the results."

Persistence through resistance is required.

**Things do not go as planned:**

> "It is not being obsessed with just moving anything as planned, it is better to take advantage of the effect of the natural adjustment on the production side."

---

### Section 3 -- What is Kanban?

**Two functions:**
1. Work instructions / signal
2. Moves hand-in-hand with actual goods

Anything with kanban functions is kanban: carrying carts, designated seats on conveyors.

> "The degree of the utilization of Kanban can indicate how high the level of management of a workplace is."

---

### Section 4 -- Kanban Rules (6 Rules)

**Rule 1:** Don't send defective products to the next production process.

**Rule 2:** The following production process comes to pick up.

**Rule 3:** Only produce the amount picked up by the next process.

**Rule 4:** Do production leveling.

**Rule 5:** Kanban is a means of fine tuning.

**Rule 6:** Stabilize and rationalize the production process.

**On Rule 1:** Leads directly to autonomation thinking.

**On Rule 2 (the key reversal):**

> "By changing the idea of 'supply' to 'pick up,' we were able to find a way to solve a difficult problem."

Three sub-rules:
1. No pickup without kanban
2. No taking more than the number of kanbans
3. Kanban must be attached to actual goods

**On Rule 6:** "Defective work" -- insufficient standardization and rationalization creates muda (waste), mura (unevenness), muri (unreasonableness).

> "Introducing kanban without actually practicing these rules will bring neither the control expected of kanban nor the cost reduction. A half-hearted introduction of kanban brings a hundred harms and not a single gain."

## Appendix: Changing the Way of Work -- Placement of Machines

**Evolution of machine placement:**

1. **Single placement** -- 1 person per machine, lots of waiting
2. **Type-specific placement** -- 2 machines per person, L-shape or triangle arrangement. Leads to "birdcage" isolated islands and overproduction of semi-processed goods
3. **Process-order placement** -- machines in production sequence, minimal walking, one-piece flow
4. **Flow production system** -- straight line, walking is part of the job, fractional workers combined across multiple lines

**The "small isolated islands" problem:** Separate machine clusters cannot achieve overall balance. They pile up WIP between processes.

**Key insight:** Work combination made it possible to adjust personnel to match required production volume -- demand-responsive staffing.
