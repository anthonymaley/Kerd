# PART 1: TPS FOUNDATION & PHILOSOPHY

## The 4P Model with Scientific Thinking at Center

The Toyota Way is organized around four interlocking areas — not a hierarchy but a system of mutual reinforcement:

| P | Domain | Essence |
|---|--------|---------|
| **Philosophy** | Long-term systems thinking | Base decisions on long-term purpose, even at expense of short-term financial goals |
| **Process** | Struggle to flow value | Create continuous process flow; eliminate stagnation and waste |
| **People** | Develop and respect | Grow leaders and teams who live the philosophy |
| **Problem Solving** | Think and act scientifically | Continuously improve toward a desired future using PDCA |

**Scientific Thinking** is the hub binding all four Ps. The 14 Principles map across these four domains.

The roof goals are achievable **only** when both pillars stand and the foundation is solid. Remove any element and the house collapses. This is why "implementing lean tools" without the foundation fails: you install parts of a pillar without a foundation.

## The Toyota Way 2001 House (Internal Philosophy Document)

Two pillars of Toyota's cultural document:
- **Respect for People**
- **Continuous Improvement**

Five foundational principles:

1. **Challenge**: "We accept challenges with a creative spirit and the courage to realize our own dreams without losing drive or energy." Every employee works toward higher performance with enthusiasm. Hoshin kanri cascades challenging goals at all levels.

2. **Kaizen**: The mandate to constantly improve for the better — NOT special events or projects. It is the daily scientific way people work toward goals using PDCA cycles. Never-ending.

3. **Genchi Genbutsu** (Go and See to Learn): Deeply understand the current condition before making a decision or trying to change something. Two aspects: (a) decisions based on observed facts, not hunches; (b) decisions made by those closest to the problem who have gone to see it with deep understanding.

4. **Teamwork and Accountability**: Individual success only within the team. Single-point accountability — one name on each action item, but that person works with the team, builds consensus, gives credit to the team.

5. **Respect and Develop People**: Toyota deliberately creates a steady flow of challenges. TPS with JIT and andon creates constant challenges on the shop floor. Investment in team members so they can be problem solvers.

## Core Philosophy: Long-Term Purpose Over Short-Term Profit

Toyota's purpose: **add value to customers and society for the long term.** Profits are an *input* to broader purpose, not the ultimate goal.

**Toyota Priority Hierarchy** (Akio Toyoda, Covid-19 crisis):
1. Safety
2. Quality
3. Volume
4. Profit-making

Key philosophical positions:
- "Do the right thing for the company, its employees, the customer, and society as a whole."
- Job security is as sacrosanct as safety: Toyota would no sooner lay off employees in a downturn than "put our sons and daughters out on the street because we lost money in the stock market."
- The company as a living sociotechnical system — not a machine with predictable linear cause-effect, but a living system requiring constant local adjustment by trained people.
- Long-term thinking does NOT mean slow thinking — it means not sacrificing the future for the quarter.

**Concrete evidence of philosophy in action — Great Recession 2008-2009:**
Toyota lost $4B in FY2009 (first loss since 1950). Two plants with 40%+ sales drops (Indiana and Texas). Response:
- Zero regular employee layoffs
- Shut plants 3 months for intensive kaizen training
- "A team" on production, "B team" on kaizen, swapping each half-shift
- Managers took voluntary pay cuts (not even announced to workforce)
- Workers took every other Friday unpaid ("shared pain")
- Used shutdown to relocate production lines, reprogram robots

Team leader quote that reveals the philosophy: "The difference between Toyota and the other companies is that instead of forcing us to go on unemployment, they are investing in us, allowing us to sharpen our minds."

## Scientific Thinking: The Central Engine

**Practical scientific thinking** (Mike Rother's formulation) has three characteristics:

1. **Acknowledging our comprehension is always incomplete and possibly wrong** — humility about what we don't know
2. **Assuming answers will be found by test rather than just deliberation** — make predictions, run experiments
3. **Appreciating that differences between prediction and actuality are a useful source of learning** — failure is data, not shame

The contrast is how most people respond to goals and problems: assume we already understand reality and the solution, neglect to test assumptions, and view failed predictions as personal failures.

**Kahneman's Fast vs. Slow Thinking** (cited by Liker):
- **Fast thinking (System 1)**: Automatic, emotional, pattern-matching, comfortable — what our brains prefer (law of least mental effort). Jumps to solutions. Creates certainty where none exists.
- **Slow thinking (System 2)**: Deliberate, systematic, scientific — what TPS demands. "Arduous, boring, even painful." Must be consciously chosen.

The biological reality: our hardware evolved for fast thinking. TPS is a system of practices and structures that forces slow thinking to become habitual through deliberate practice — until it feels natural.

**Toyota's 1973 TPS Manual**: "On the shop floor it is important to start with the actual phenomenon and search for the root cause in order to solve the problem. We must emphasize 'getting the facts.'"

## Organic vs. Mechanistic Lean

| Mechanistic | Organic |
|---|---|
| Project-based | Purpose-driven |
| Expert-driven | Engaging people |
| Top-down | Coaching |
| Tool deployment | A journey |
| Fast, quick results | Robust, sustainable |

**Enabling Bureaucracy** (Paul Adler, NUMMI study): A fourth organizational type combining mechanistic structure with organic social enabling. Not coercive (Taylorist control) but empowering — structure serves people, not controls them. Toyota's TPS is the prime example.

The enabling bureaucracy insight from Adler: "Formal work standards developed by industrial engineers and imposed on workers are alienating. But procedures that are designed by the workers themselves in a continuous and successful effort to improve productivity, quality, skills, and understanding can humanize even the most disciplined forms of bureaucracy."

# PART 2: THE 14 PRINCIPLES

## PHILOSOPHY (P1)

### Principle 1 — Base Your Management Decisions on Long-Term Systems Thinking, Even at the Expense of Short-Term Financial Goals

**Essence**: The organization exists for a purpose beyond profit. Long-term survival and value creation are the mission. Profit is a byproduct and input, not the goal.

**Problem-Solving Implications**:
- Team members surface problems rather than hide them because they trust the company
- Job security enables risk-taking and experimentation
- Leaders ask "how are you problem solving to get to root cause?" not "why did you miss production?"
- Scientific thinking skills (challenge, kaizen, genchi genbutsu) are fundamentally problem-solving capabilities
- Systems thinking: understand that parts interact in non-linear ways; plans will never execute as written; trained people must make informed local adjustments

---

## PROCESS (P2–P8)

### Principle 2 — Connect People and Processes Through Continuous Process Flow to Bring Problems to the Surface

**Essence**: Flow value to each customer, ideally one by one, without stagnation. One-piece flow is a vision to struggle toward, not a tool to implement once.

**Key Insight**: One-piece flow's primary benefit is not efficiency — it is that it forces problems to the surface so people must solve them and grow. It is deliberately "a bad system that will break."

**The Math That Matters**:
- Batch & queue: 4 processes at 90% uptime = 90% output (buffers hide inefficiency)
- One-piece flow: 90% × 90% × 90% × 90% = **65.6% output** unless every problem is solved

Toyota's 97% OPR (Operational Ratio) is achieved not by preventing problems but by solving them continuously. That is the paradox that explains TPS.

**One-Piece Flow as Dependent Variable** (Rother's insight): Usually thought of as independent variable (implement it to get results). Better to think of it as a **dependent variable** — you get closer to one-piece flow as you scientifically solve problems one by one. As you get closer, the chain tightens, exposes new problems. Repeated virtuous cycle.

**8 Benefits of One-Piece Flow**:
1. Builds in quality (defects detected in 2 units, not 21)
2. Creates real flexibility (short lead times = faster response)
3. Creates higher productivity (eliminates hidden wastes)
4. Frees up floor space (eliminates inventory piles)
5. Improves safety (eliminates forklifts, heavy lifting)
6. Improves morale (value-added work creates accomplishment)
7. Reduces cost of inventory (frees cash flow)
8. Unleashes creativity of people (problems surface and challenge thinking)

**Minoura on intentional confusion**: "When they run one-piece production, they can't have the quantity that they want so everybody gets frustrated and doesn't know what to do. But then, within that, they have to find ways to think: what is the way to get the quantity? That is the true essence of TPS and, in that sense, we create confusion so we have to do something different."

---

### Principle 3 — Use "Pull" Systems to Avoid Overproduction

**Essence**: Give customers (internal or external) what they want, when they want it, in the amount they want — based on actual demand signals, not forecasts. Overproduction is the fundamental waste.

**The Kanban Paradox**: TPS experts get "very impatient and even irritated when they hear people rave about kanban." Kanban is organized waste. The real purpose is to **eliminate the kanban** — reduce inventory to expose and solve problems, approaching one-piece flow.

**How to use kanban as a problem-surfacing tool**: Take one kanban out of the system and throw it away. 25% less inventory → system stressed faster when machine goes down → forces process improvements. This deliberate stress is problem-surfacing by design.

**"Flow where you can, pull where you must"** (Rother): Aim for one-piece flow; when not possible, use pull with inventory buffers; shrink those buffers over time.

**Problem-Solving Implications**:
- Each kanban removed reveals the next problem hiding behind inventory
- Pull systems create immediate feedback — problems surface within hours, not weeks
- Distributed local control: Toyota's systems break processes into smaller parts, distribute control to local customers, create small feedback loops based on most recent information

---

### Principle 4 — Level Out the Workload, Like the Tortoise, Not the Hare (Heijunka)

**Essence**: Level production by both volume AND product mix. Without leveling, eliminating muda actually hurts productivity. The tortoise beats the hare.

**The Three Ms** — must address ALL three simultaneously:
- **Muda**: Non-value-added waste (the 7 wastes)
- **Mura**: Unevenness; irregular production schedule or fluctuating volumes → creates the bullwhip effect through supply chain
- **Muri**: Overburden; pushing people or equipment beyond natural limits → causes safety/quality problems and breakdowns

**Critical accountability distinction**: "Muda is viewed as something that can be worked on by the frontline work group, but overburden and unevenness are the responsibility of management." Management owns muri and mura; team members own muda.

**SMED** (Single-Minute Exchange of Dies, Shingo):
- Move all possible work to **external setup** (done while machine runs)
- Minimize **internal setup** (work requiring machine to be stopped)
- Result: Several-hundred-ton presses reduced from hours to minutes

**Why heijunka is the foundation**: Leveling → enables takt → enables standardized work and line balancing → enables leveled pull from upstream → minimum inventory, minimum cost, best quality. Without leveling, the whole system destabilizes.

---

### Principle 5 — Work to Establish Standardized Processes as the Foundation for Continuous Improvement

**Essence**: Standardized work is not a rigid Taylorist control mechanism — it is the foundation for continuous improvement. You cannot improve a process that is not first stabilized.

**Toyota's 3 Elements of Standardized Work** (per Fujio Cho):
1. **Takt time**: Time required to complete one job at the pace of customer demand
2. **Sequence of processes**: The order in which operations are performed
3. **Standardized stock on hand**: How much WIP inventory the worker needs to accomplish the standardized work

**The Living Standard**: Standardized work is "today's best-known way, which can be improved tomorrow." Henry Ford: "If you think of standardization as the best you know today, but which is to be improved tomorrow — you get somewhere. But if you think of standards as confining, then progress stops."

**Workers own their standards**: Toyota turned classical industrial engineering on its head — work groups are responsible for designing and continuously improving their own work. "We gave the stopwatch to the work groups."

**How standardized work enables problem solving**:
1. Provides the baseline — "what should be happening"
2. When defect occurs: Was standardized work followed? If not → correct behavior. If yes → change the standardized work.
3. Work-balance chart reveals overburdened/underloaded steps → targets for kaizen
4. Standardized worksheet (posted outward facing manager) allows audit compliance visually → deviations are problems to solve

---

### Principle 6 — Build a Culture of Stopping to Identify Out-of-Standard Conditions and Build in Quality (Jidoka)

**Essence**: Build in quality at the source. Stop and fix problems immediately. Never pass a defect forward. In-station quality is far more effective and less costly than inspect-and-repair.

**The Cho Paradox**: "If you are not shutting down the assembly plant, it means that you have no problems. All manufacturing plants have problems. So you must be hiding your problems." — Cho to Scaffede (GM veteran)

**Gary Convis**: "There are two ways you can get in trouble here: one is you don't come to work, and two is you don't pull the cord if you've got a problem."

**The foundational problem-solving cycle for quality problems**:
1. Team member sees out-of-standard condition → pulls andon cord
2. Team leader responds within 10-20 seconds
3. Assess: Can I fix it now? Should line stop?
4. If line stops: fix problem or contain it
5. Team leader examines andon pulls offline → selects most common/serious ones
6. Root cause problem solving → countermeasure → standardized work update → poka-yoke design

**First question when any defect is found**: "Was standardized work followed?"
- If no → correct behavior
- If yes and defects still occur → modify standardized work

**Quality ownership is universal**: "The sense of accountability to ensure quality at each station is really critical." Every team member is an inspector. Quality is not a department.

---

### Principle 7 — Use Visual Control to Support People in Decision-Making and Problem Solving

**Essence**: Visual control means any communication device that tells at a glance how work should be done and whether it is deviating from the standard. Not about making things look neat — about making problems immediately visible so they can be solved.

**The Biology**: "Hear a piece of information, and three days later you'll remember 10% of it. Add a picture and you'll remember 65%." (John Medina, Brain Rules)

**5S IS NOT lean**: Many companies think 5S IS lean. "It is lipstick on a pig" if applied to a mass production system without stable processes. 5S should support smooth flow to takt, not organize waste neatly.

**Visual management as a problem-detection mechanism**:
- Any tool out of its shadow board position = visible problem
- Inventory outside min/max boundaries = visible problem
- Process control board behind schedule = visible problem requiring immediate investigation
- Andon light = visible problem requiring team leader response
- All deviations become immediately apparent to anyone walking through

**Key insight on digitization**: "Using an electronic monitor does not work if only one person uses that information. Visual management charts must allow for communication and sharing." — Ichiro Suzuki (chief engineer, first Lexus). If plans are hidden in a password-protected database, genchi genbutsu of planning cannot happen.

---

### Principle 8 — Adopt and Adapt Technology That Supports Your People and Processes

**Essence**: Technology serves people and processes, not the reverse. Pull technology to address real problems; do not push it because it is the latest fad. Automate only what is already a well-understood, stable process.

**Four skills required before automating** (Mitsuru Kawai):
1. Visualize production
2. Develop explicit knowledge of the process
3. Standardize the knowledge
4. Develop intelligent automation through kaizen

**The principle**: First work out the manual process. Build maximum flexibility so kaizen can continue as business changes. Then automate. Never automate a process you don't deeply understand manually first.

**Akio Toyoda**: "I believe that the more automation advances, the more the ability of the people using it will be put to the test. Machines cannot improve unless people do, too."

**"Electronic wallpaper"** (Dave Grimmer, Denso): Data dashboards and displays with no corresponding action taken. The failure mode of Industry 4.0 implementation without TPS thinking.

**IoT and fast PDCA**: "We can't afford to have PDCA that takes three weeks anymore. We want a PDCA done before the end of that shift." — Raja Shembekar (Denso VP). IoT enables completing a full PDCA cycle within a single shift.

## PEOPLE (P9–P11)

### Principle 9 — Grow Leaders Who Thoroughly Understand the Work, Live the Philosophy, and Teach It to Others

**Essence**: Leaders must be grown from within, thoroughly understand the actual work, live the Toyota Way values, and actively teach those values to others. A leader's primary job is to develop people.

**The Ohno Circle**: Taiichi Ohno's training device — drew a circle on the shop floor and had students stand in it for hours (sometimes all day) observing, without explanation. Purpose: develop the skill of deep observation and teach thinking for oneself.

**Hourensou**: Daily reporting, updating, and consulting system. "Hou" (report) + "ren" (periodic updates) + "sou" (consult/advise). Toyota managers require subordinates to send daily bullet-point journal entries. Creates efficient information sharing, just-in-time coaching, and cross-functional learning.

**Level 5 Leader** (Collins, cited): Leader with intense professional will + personal humility; grows from within; looks in mirror to assign blame, out window to assign credit; obsessive about knowing the business in detail. Toyota's leadership profile matches this archetype.

**"Before we build cars, we build people."**

**New engineer onboarding**: Sell cars door-to-door for one to two months as part of freshman training — visceral understanding of the customer is non-negotiable.

**Performance appraisals**: More than half based on competencies (how), not just results (what). Toyota Global Appraisal Process (GAP) Competency 1: "Accurate grasp of the situation: Dig deep, go and see, listen, use facts and data."

**Ohno's teaching method**: "Try something and do it now." Failure acceptable if the student is learning. Cho on Ohno: "He could be very critical when he saw something wrong. But then at the end of the day he would explain why he was critical. Everyone, even the junior workers, appreciated that he was trying to teach them."

---

### Principle 10 — Develop Exceptional People and Teams Who Follow Your Company's Philosophy

**Essence**: Respect for people means challenging them to grow. Servant leadership flips the org chart: value-added workers are at the top, supported by layers beneath.

**Team Structure**:
- 4 team members per **Team Leader (TL)** — hourly, first response to andon, keeps line running, no disciplinary authority
- 4 team leaders per **Group Leader (GL)** — first management level; runs a small business: HR, training, performance review, safety, coordination of major process changes, hoshin kanri inputs

**Group Leader is "the managing director of their own business"**: Can perform any job on the line. Coaches TBP projects. Leads kaizen. Owns KPI boards. Conducts power meetings daily.

**Job security as the foundation of trust**: Without job security, team members will not surface problems (fear of looking incompetent), will not participate in kaizen (fear of working themselves out of a job), and will not trust leadership's stated intentions.

**Toyota's 80,000 suggestions per year** at Georgetown; 99% implemented — enabled by close coaching and small-group structure. Not driven by financial incentives — driven by intrinsic motivation (meaningful work, recognition by peers and leaders).

**Extrinsic rewards hurt creative performance** (Duncker candle experiment, cited): Pay-per-idea programs undermine the intrinsic motivation that drives genuine kaizen. Toyota relies on recognition, not cash bonuses for suggestions.

---

### Principle 11 — Respect Your Value Chain Partners by Challenging Them and Helping Them Improve

**Essence**: Treat suppliers, dealers, and service providers as extensions of Toyota — challenge them with high standards, help them improve, never bully them.

**"Achievement of business performance by the parent company through bullying suppliers is totally alien to the spirit of the Toyota Production System."** — Taiichi Ohno

**The Aisin Fire (1997)**: Sole-source p-valve factory burned. 200 suppliers self-organized in 2 days to restart production. This was only possible because Toyota had spent decades building deep mutual trust and shared TPS knowledge. Toyota's reflection: not to abandon JIT, but to avoid sole-sourcing critical parts to one location.

**Value chain needs hierarchy** (Liker): Foundation = fair/equitable commercial relations → stable/reliable processes → enabling systems → mutual learning. Cannot get to the top without satisfying lower levels.

**Target cost system**: Toyota gives first-tier suppliers a "checkbook" (maximum allowed cost). Supplier designs collaboratively to hit that target. Post-launch: Toyota requests annual price reductions, assuming kaizen will continually reduce cost. This assumes a partnership, not an adversarial negotiation.

## PROBLEM SOLVING (P12–P14)

### Principle 12 — Observe Deeply and Learn Iteratively (PDCA) to Meet Each Challenge

**Essence**: Toyota is a learning organization built on scientific thinking — observing, trying, reflecting, and learning — developed one person at a time through practice and coaching. Certainty is the enemy; uncertainty is a fact of life to navigate through experiments.

*All major problem-solving tools reside in this principle — see Part 3 for complete descriptions.*

---

### Principle 13 — Focus the Improvement Energy of Your People Through Aligned Goals at All Levels (Hoshin Kanri)

**Essence**: Hoshin kanri is not a target-cascading tool — it is a mountain-climbing process. Senior leaders act as sherpas guiding execution, not boardroom overseers reviewing results.

**Annual Process Flow**:
1. Environmental scan + strategic plan (top of organization)
2. Q3: Individual units begin hansei (reflection on previous year) and preparation
3. Q4: President's address launches hoshin planning with global hoshin items
4. 3 months of planning: communicating horizontally and vertically, building consensus (nemawashi), committing to targets
5. End result: A3 reports cascading through organization, increasingly specific as you go down
6. Execution = mountain climbing: many PDCA cycles facing unanticipated obstacles
7. Two major reviews: midyear and year-end; but constant local reviews throughout
8. Daily standups at all management levels next to visual boards

**Toyota's planning hierarchy**:
- 10-year global vision (revised every ~decade)
- 5-year business plan
- 1–3-year breakthrough objectives
- Annual hoshin items
- Work-group KPI targets

**Mechanistic vs. Organic hoshin**:
- Mechanistic: CEO strategy → cascade down → executives review results from boardroom. Only see what's above the waterline.
- Organic (Toyota): Leaders climb down from the mountain top during execution and act as sherpas — guiding, supporting, developing. The iceberg metaphor: leaders must manage what's below the waterline, not just report results.

**Causal reasoning in hoshin**: The critical thinking discipline — not "what are my targets" but "what do I need to work on to help my boss achieve their targets?" This is causal decomposition, not proportional target allocation.

---

### Principle 14 — Learn Your Way to the Future Through Bold Strategy, Some Large Leaps, and Many Small Steps

**Essence**: Strategy sets the direction; execution is a series of iterative learning experiments. Neither is a spectator sport.

**Strategy execution follows improvement kata pattern**: Start with strategic vision → identify first target condition → experiment → reflect → set next target condition → repeat.

**"Don't fall into the trap of thinking that just because you have a well-articulated strategy, with informative figures and charts, that you are done. You have only started."**

# PART 3: PROBLEM-SOLVING FRAMEWORKS & TOOLS

## PDCA (Plan-Do-Check-Act)

The cornerstone of Toyota's approach to continuous improvement and scientific thinking, derived from Deming.

**Two versions — one scientific, one counterfeit:**

| Aspect | False PDCA (mechanistic) | True PDCA (scientific) |
|--------|--------------------------|------------------------|
| **Plan** | Commit to a known solution | Set direction and form hypotheses |
| **Do** | Implement the solution | Run experiments |
| **Check** | Confirm the solution worked | Compare prediction to actual — what did we learn? |
| **Act** | Broadly deploy | Refine and set next target condition; cycle repeats |
| **Metaphor** | GPS (certain destination, certain path) | Compass (direction known, path discovered) |
| **Assumption** | Certainty before action | Uncertainty is the starting condition |
| **Point of commitment** | At maximum uncertainty (the planning stage) | After evidence from experiments |

**PDCA within the TPS House**:
- Plan = develop the vision of one-piece flow
- Do = experiment toward target conditions
- Check = jidoka surfaces problems (andon as the check mechanism)
- Act = standardize new practice; cycle repeats

**SDCA (Standard-Do-Check-Act)** — The companion to PDCA:
- Used for deviations from existing standards at the work group level
- A problem is a deviation from standard → SDCA
- SDCA removes small rocks; PDCA removes big boulders
- Used together at all levels; PDCA adds new standards, SDCA maintains them

## 5 Whys (Five Whys)

Toyota's root-cause analysis method — iteratively asking "Why?" to move from symptom to root cause.

**Critical nuance**: "Root cause" lies hidden beyond the "source." The source is where you can easily point; the root cause requires deep investigation past it.

**Not a formula**: "Five" is not literal. The real lesson is: go to the gemba and get facts before asking why; wipe away preconceptions of what is happening and what the solution is.

**Ohno's instruction**: "Repeat why five times to every matter" — but observe the production floor "without preconceptions and with a blank mind."

**Classic example (Ohno)**:
- Oil spill on floor → Why? Gasket failed → Why? Gasket not durable enough → Why? Gasket design not specified properly → Why? Machinist purchasing agent not trained in specifications → Why? Purchasing agent evaluation criteria don't include technical specs.
- **Solution at root**: Change how purchasing agents are evaluated. A surface fix (replace gasket) would have recurred indefinitely.

**The 5 Whys → 5 Whos failure mode**: When people are defensive, 5 Whys degenerates into 5 Whos (finding blame). Creating a psychologically safe environment is a prerequisite. The investigation focuses on system failure, never individual failure.

**John Shook**: "Genchi genbutsu means confirm what's really happening. Five whys means pursue understanding of causality. Together they must remain foundational — regardless of digital tools."

**Blue baby hospital example**: Intelligent people in a conference room fabricated root causes without evidence. Going to gemba with blank minds revealed: wrong sensors (adult sensors on babies), administrative assistant turning off all alarms assuming false alarms, no voice follow-up protocol. Inexpensive process changes fixed a 150-second response time problem down to 20 seconds. No new computer system needed.

## A3 Thinking

**What it is**: An 11×17-inch (A3 paper size) single-page document that tells a complete story using bullet points, charts, and graphs.

**What it is NOT**: A template or a report format. The A3 is an artifact for making the learner's thinking visible so the coach can teach. "A hook to assist a manager in coaching others." — John Shook

**The real A3 process**: Many revisions (sometimes 10+), nemawashi, torn-up versions, challenges from coaches, red-ink markups. The work happens in the struggle, not in the final document.

Shook: "We would rewrite A3s 10 times or more. We would write and revise them, tear them up and start over, discuss them and curse them, all as ways of clarifying our own thinking, learning from others, informing and teaching others."

**Types of A3**:
- Problem-solving A3
- Proposal A3
- Status report A3
- Information-sharing A3
- Hoshin planning A3

**All good A3s have PDCA built in** — they tell the story of current condition, gap, target, hypotheses, experiments, and learning.

**How A3s cascade through hoshin kanri**: Each level's A3 becomes more specific and actionable. Higher levels: conceptual direction. Lower levels: specific daily actions. The cascade creates vertical alignment while enabling horizontal visibility.

**The coaching use of A3**: At NUMMI, every major breakdown (>30 min) generated an A3 breakdown report. Ito marked up Convis's A3s with red ink on weak points — making the engineer's thinking visible and teaching scientific problem-solving through the artifact.

**Why paper, not software**: If plans are hidden in a password-protected database, genchi genbutsu of planning cannot happen. The physical visibility of information at the gemba IS a form of visual management that triggers questions. Andy Heaphy (TMUK GM): "To see everything in an easy way is very hard to do on a computer... it makes you wonder, what is the status of that? You are asking that question. If it is hidden from you, what triggers you to ask the question?"

## Toyota Business Practices (TBP) — 8-Step Problem Solving

Introduced by Fujio Cho after Toyota Way 2001 to give people a framework for developing scientific thinking through practice on real problems. Not a rigid method — a developmental framework.

**8 Steps:**

**Step 1: Clarify the Problem**
Start with a big challenge. Grasp the current condition. Define the ideal condition. Visualize the large gap. **No root cause analysis yet.** This step is about understanding what kind of problem you actually have, not jumping to causes.

**Step 2: Break Down the Problem**
Go to the gemba to understand. Break the large gap into smaller, tractable sub-problems. Prioritize one to start. This sub-problem selection is itself a strategic judgment: which sub-problem, if solved, will produce the most learning or impact?

**Step 3: Set a Target**
"With enthusiasm and commitment, set challenging targets" for the prioritized sub-problem. Make the target specific, measurable, and time-bound. This creates the hypothesis-testing framework for the experiment.

**Step 4: Analyze the Root Cause**
Go to the gemba. Investigate based on actual facts. **Not asking why five times in a conference room.** Direct observation of the actual phenomenon precedes causal analysis.

**Step 5: Develop Countermeasures**
"Broadly consider all stakeholders and risks." Think creatively beyond preconceived ideas. Engage stakeholders, build consensus (nemawashi/signoffs). Multiple countermeasures possible; evaluate based on predicted impact and feasibility.

**Step 6: See Countermeasures Through**
Collaborative implementation. Inform, report, consult key stakeholders. The implementation itself is an experiment — watch carefully for unintended consequences.

**Step 7: Monitor Both Results and Processes**
Evaluate from customer, company, and personal-development perspectives. Did the countermeasure achieve the target? If not, what did you learn? Was the process (thinking, collaboration, scientific rigor) sound?

**Step 8: Standardize Successful Processes**
New processes become the standard and are shared via yokoten. The learner is not done until the knowledge spreads. This is the "Act" of PDCA made concrete.

**Critical vocabulary**: "Countermeasure" vs. "solution." "There are no solutions, only countermeasures." A countermeasure is a hypothesis that might help reduce the gap. Proven countermeasures become standards — "the best we know today until we find a better one."

**TBP is iterative, not linear**: After addressing sub-problem 1, revisit the current condition, select the next sub-problem, continue. The 8 steps repeat at smaller and smaller scales.

**TBP deployment at Toyota**:
- Started at senior executive level with Fujio Cho as examiner; ~80% sent back for more work on first attempt
- Cascades down: each level coaches the next; 8 years to reach group leader level at one plant
- Report-out on a strict 15-minute A3 to a panel of three; pass/fail with "ready to mentor" rating
- Third phase: OJD (On-the-Job Development) — train the coach by having them coach a TBP project while being coached by a "second coach" (meta-coaching)

## Toyota Kata (Improvement Kata / Coaching Kata)

Developed by Mike Rother from 6 years of research into Toyota's management routines.

**Kata** = form/way of doing things + pattern of movements practiced to develop fundamental skill (from martial arts).

### Improvement Kata (IK) — 4 Steps:

**Step 1: Set the Direction or Challenge**
Usually set by management. Often seems impossible — forces breakdown and learning. Typically 6 months to 1 year out. The challenge provides the compass direction; it does NOT specify the path.

**Step 2: Grasp the Current Condition**
Where are we now? Calculate statistics. Observe the current process. Make run charts. Document variation across trials. Identify reasons for variation. This must be current and factual, not based on memory or reports.

**Step 3: Establish the Next Target Condition**
A significant step beyond current condition toward the challenge. Includes target (outcome metric) + condition (desired process characteristics and process metric). Typically 1–4 weeks out. **Critical: do NOT plot all target conditions in advance.** Only establish the next one. The path beyond your threshold of knowledge is unknown.

**Step 4: Experiment**
Test one factor at a time. Predict what will happen. Run the experiment. Reflect on what was learned. Repeat rapid PDCA cycles until target condition is reached. Then set the next target condition.

### Coaching Kata (CK) — 5-Question Starter Kata:

These are the five questions a coach asks the learner at every coaching cycle:
1. What is the target condition?
2. What is the actual condition now?
3. What obstacles are preventing you from reaching the target condition? Which one are you addressing now?
4. What is your next step (experiment)? What do you expect?
5. How quickly can we go and see what we have learned from taking that step?

**Key distinctions**:
- IK is more fundamental and meta than TBP; it represents the underlying pattern of scientific thinking that makes any problem-solving method more effective
- IK assumes uncertainty: "navigate with a compass, not a GPS"
- Starter kata are training wheels; the goal is to internalize the scientific-thinking pattern until it becomes habitual
- Neurological basis: repeated practice creates neural pathways; slow thinking becomes more natural with practice

## Hansei (Reflection)

**Definition**: "Being honest about your own weaknesses." Deeper than casual reflection. Not a meeting format — a mindset and an attitude.

**Three-part process**:
1. Feel sincerely sad/concerned about the mistake (emotional acknowledgment, not just intellectual noting)
2. Create a future plan to avoid recurrence
3. Sincerely commit to never making this type of mistake again

**Hansei-kai**: A reflection meeting held even after successes to identify what could have been better.

**George Yamashina**: "Without hansei it is impossible to have kaizen. Hansei is a mindset, an attitude. Hansei and kaizen go hand in hand."

**Bruce Brownlee**: "If you are recognizing your weaknesses with sincerity, it is a high level of strength. But it does not end there. How do you change to overcome those weaknesses? That is at the root of the very notion of kaizen."

**Where it appears in the system**:
- Q3 hoshin kanri planning: formal hansei on previous year's plan
- After each project milestone
- In A3 coaching sessions (red-ink moments)
- After recalls and quality crises (Toyota's annual February 24 quality day after the Lexus recall)
- After every TBP project (Step 7: monitor results AND process)

**Toyota first introduced hansei to US managers at TTC in 1994**, linked to hoshin kanri and A3 thinking. It was not spontaneously absorbed — it was deliberately taught.

## Nemawashi

**Definition**: "Going around the roots" — preparing the ground before transplanting a tree. Building broad consensus before a formal decision.

**The process**:
- Informal conversations with all stakeholders with a stake or special knowledge
- Gather input from diverse perspectives
- Address concerns and objections before the formal meeting
- Build consensus so the formal decision is already agreed before it's announced

**Critical distinction**: Consensus does NOT mean 100% agreement from everyone. It means everyone's input has been considered. Everyone supports the final decision 100% once made, even if they had a different view.

**Why it appears slow but is actually fast**: A decision reached through nemawashi takes longer to plan but executes at the speed of full commitment. A decision made without nemawashi executes at the speed of reluctant compliance — slow, full of rework.

**Where it appears**:
- TBP Step 5 (develop countermeasures): stakeholder signoffs required before implementation
- Hoshin kanri planning: catchball with all levels
- Product development: K4 (kozokeikaku) document circulated to 100+ people
- Set-based design: multiple stakeholders narrow alternatives together
- Any complex organizational decision

## Yokoten

**Definition** (Alistair Norval): "Horizontal and peer-to-peer, with the expectation that people go see for themselves and learn how another area did kaizen and then improve on those kaizen ideas in the application to their local problems. It's not a vertical, top-down requirement to 'copy exactly.' Nor is it a 'best practices' or 'benchmarking' approach. Rather, people are encouraged to go see for themselves and return to their own area to add their own wisdom and ideas."

**Japanese literal meaning**: "Across everywhere" / "spread horizontally."

**Why this matters**:
- Reflects the Toyota epistemology: understanding cannot be transferred; it must be earned through direct observation and thinking
- Respects the intelligence of the receiving party — they adapt, not copy
- Prevents the "copy-and-paste" failure mode of lean tool deployment

**Step 8 of TBP**: "Standardize successful processes — the learner is not done until the new process is shared with others who might benefit." Yokoten is built into the problem-solving process.

---

## Genchi Genbutsu (Go and See)

**Definition**: "The actual place and actual thing." Go directly to the source to find facts. Make decisions based on observed reality, not reports or theory.

**Two aspects**:
1. Decisions based on **observed facts**, not hunches, assumptions, or perceptions
2. Decisions in the hands of those **closest to the problem** who have gone to see it with deep understanding of causes and impact of proposed solutions

**Ohno's rule**: The greatest sin was to take things for granted and assume you know. "Back to the gemba!"

**In the digital age** (Akio Toyoda): "Some things can only be felt when you are at the gemba... For those things where you need to feel the actual products, the actual people, that should be done at the actual site."

**John Shook**: "Genchi genbutsu means grasping the real situation. How you get facts and grasp the situation is actually secondary. It doesn't mean you must always literally go and see. It means confirm what's really happening."

**The Ohno Circle** as a teaching device: Students stood in circles drawn on the shop floor for hours — sometimes an entire day. The explicit teaching: the discipline of deep observation without preconceptions. The implicit teaching: the gemba is where truth lives.

## Andon System (Fixed-Position Line-Stop)

**What it is**: Toyota's structured immediate-response system for out-of-standard conditions at the point of production.

**How it works** (step by step):
1. Team member sees any deviation from standard → pulls andon cord → workstation lights **yellow**
2. Line continues moving (allows team member to finish current vehicle)
3. **Team leader** has until vehicle enters next workstation zone (10-20 seconds) to respond
4. Team leader can: (a) fix it immediately, (b) cancel the stop (cord pulled again), or (c) allow line to stop
5. If allowed → workstation turns **red** → **line SEGMENT stops** (not entire line)
6. Assembly line divided into segments with small buffers (7-10 cars) between them
7. Adjacent segments keep running for 7-10 minutes before shutting down
8. Rarely does entire plant shut down

**Why this matters for problem solving**: The andon is the "Check" in the TPS-wide PDCA cycle. Without it, problems are invisible until defects reach customers. With it, every abnormality is surfaced immediately, responded to within seconds, and becomes a learning opportunity.

**The hardware-store version**: Toyota mentor drove a manager who'd spec'd an expensive electronic andon to a hardware store and bought red, yellow, and green flags. Said: "Andon." The principle is what matters, not the technology. The system only works when people understand the importance of surfacing problems and have a problem-solving process to follow.

## Poka-Yoke (Error Proofing)

**Definition**: Creative devices making errors nearly impossible. "Mistake proofing" or "fool proofing."

**Each poka-yoke device has its own standard form**:
1. Problem the device addresses
2. Emergency alarm that will sound
3. Action to take in an emergency
4. Method and frequency for confirming the device is working
5. Method for quality check if the device breaks down

The standard form IS part of the poka-yoke — without confirming the device works, you have false confidence.

**Examples**:
- Light curtain over cotter pin tray: if curtain not broken (operator didn't reach for pin), line stops and alarm sounds
- Key kanban: color-coded metal kanban acts as a physical key that opens only the correct parts bin
- Shadow board for tools: missing tool is immediately visible

**27 poka-yoke devices on the front axle line at NUMMI alone** — a window into the density of error-proofing in a mature TPS implementation.

## 5S Methodology

**Five principles of workplace organization**:
1. **Sort (Seiri)**: Keep only what is needed; dispose of what is not. Red-tag everything questionable.
2. **Straighten/Orderliness (Seiton)**: "A place for everything and everything in its place." Shadow boards, labels, floor markings.
3. **Shine/Cleanliness (Seiso)**: **Cleaning IS inspection.** Exposes abnormal and pre-failure conditions. The act of cleaning builds process knowledge.
4. **Standardize (Seiketsu)**: Develop systems and procedures to maintain the first 3 Ss. Checklists, audit schedules, standard methods.
5. **Sustain/Self-discipline (Shitsuke)**: Ongoing continuous improvement as conditions change.

**Toyota actually uses 4S** — drops Sustain: "assumed; without sustaining, why bother?" Sustaining is not a fifth step; it's the consequence of the first four being genuinely understood.

**5S Audit**: Periodic scores for different groups. Deviations = problems to solve through root cause analysis ("What in the system allowed that to happen?"). NOT a compliance exercise — a problem-surfacing tool.

---

## Heijunka (Production Leveling)

**Definition**: Leveling production by both volume AND product mix over a fixed period to create smooth flow.

**Heijunka Box**: Visual scheduling device with time slots (e.g., 8:00 a.m., 8:10 a.m., etc.). Kanban cards placed in slots tell the cell what to make at what pace. Forces adherence to leveled sequence.

**Mixed-model production sequence**: Instead of A, A, A, A, B, B, B, B → A, B, A, B, A, B... Spread different product types to a predictable repeating sequence.

**Why heijunka is hardest to implement but most important**: "In general, when you try to apply the TPS, the first thing you have to do is to even out or level the production. And that is the responsibility primarily of production control or production management people." — Fujio Cho

---

## Value Stream Mapping (VSM)

**Definition**: Method to understand material and information flow in work processes.

**Current-state map**: Shows how value flows and the wastes blocking it — all material flows, information flows, cycle times, changeover times, inventory levels.

**Future-state map**: Aspirational vision of how the value stream should work — fewer buffers, more flow, pull signals, leveled schedule.

**Purpose**: The map is not the destination — it is the starting point for a systematic problem-solving plan. The gap between current and future state IS the improvement agenda.

## Floor Management Development System (FMDS)

Toyota's standard system for work groups:
- Daily stand-up meetings (power meetings) in front of visual boards
- Clearly defined roles and responsibilities
- KPI boards at every level
- Training for team leaders and group leaders
- Cadence of review: daily (power meeting), monthly (trend analysis), quarterly (hoshin review)

**KPI board structure** (standard global layout):
- Safety/Environment → Quality → Productivity → Cost → HR Development
- Main KPIs = outcomes from manager's hoshin
- Sub-KPIs = causal factors that influence main KPIs (group leaders work on sub-KPIs, not just raw outcomes)
- Focus items = specific actions to improve sub-KPIs

---

## Hoshin Kanri (Policy Deployment)

**Definition**: Management system keeping the entire organization aligned and focused on achieving well-defined shared goals. **Both** a planning/results tool **and** a process for developing people through coaching.

**Origin**: Introduced at Toyota in early 1960s as part of total quality control. Toyota won the Deming Prize for TQM in 1965 using it.

**Catchball**: The give-and-take exchange of targets and ideas between organizational levels. Not just a planning-stage meeting — an ongoing coaching and learning process throughout the year. Each level asks: "What do I need to work on to help my boss achieve their targets?" — causal reasoning, not proportional target splitting.

**X-matrix** (used by some companies, not Toyota itself): Top-level planning tool linking 3–5-year breakthroughs, annual objectives, improvement priorities, targets, and responsible people on a single page.

**"No battle plan survives first contact with the enemy"**: Once deployment starts, the plan becomes a starting point for learning, not a script. The skill is in navigating to the goal through PDCA cycles, not in executing the original plan.

---

## Quality Circles

**Definition**: Offline team activity (often after hours) where intact work groups led by team leaders solve quality, productivity, or safety problems using a simplified 6-step problem-solving process.

**Active in every Toyota plant globally.** 6-month projects. Led by team leader. Group leader coaches. Best projects presented regionally and globally; winners go to Japan.

**The 6-step simplified process** for quality circles:
1. Theme selection
2. Current condition analysis
3. Target setting
4. Root cause analysis
5. Countermeasure implementation
6. Standardize and evaluate

**Why this matters**: Quality circles take the TBP 8-step framework and make it accessible to hourly team members. Every team member at Toyota has direct experience leading or participating in structured problem solving.

---

## Jishuken (Voluntary Study Groups)

**Definition**: "Voluntary study groups" started in 1977 by Toyota's Operations Management Consulting Division (OMCD). Groups of 4–7 suppliers meet by geography/part type; TPS trainers rotate across companies on 3–4 month radical-transformation projects (not incremental improvement); representatives visit each other and make recommendations.

**"Compulsory in Japan; voluntary/supported in the US."** The word "voluntary" is somewhat ironic — Toyota expects participation from core suppliers.

# PART 4: KEY TPS VOCABULARY

## Complete Japanese/Technical Terms Reference

**Andon** — "Light" or "lamp." Signal device (cord, button, light board) for team members to alert leaders of problems threatening safety, quality, or productivity. Equipment also has automatic sensing and signaling built in. The mechanism that makes jidoka operational in an assembly setting.

**Autonomation (Jidoka)** — Equipment endowed with human intelligence to stop itself when detecting a problem. Frees the operator for value-added work and problem solving. Attributed to Sakichi Toyoda's automatic loom that stopped when a thread broke.

**BAMA** — Bluegrass Automotive Manufacturers Association. Toyota's North American core supplier association; regional supplier groups participating in jishuken and TPS development.

**Catchball** — Give-and-take exchange of targets and ideas between organizational levels in hoshin planning. Metaphor for iterative back-and-forth dialogue. Real catchball is an ongoing process, not just a planning-stage meeting.

**Chaku chaku** — "Load-load." A semi-automated cell where the person loads parts into self-cycling machines sequentially. Both hands used. Machines auto-eject. Can be augmented with simple pick-and-place robots.

**Countermeasure** — A hypothesis that might reduce the gap between desired and current condition. Tested, not assumed. Proven countermeasures become standards — "the best we know today until we find a better one." Deliberate contrast with "solution," which implies finality and certainty.

**Dunning-Kruger effect** (cited) — Beginners overestimate their ability. Dangerous for TPS learning: those who think they already know won't submit to the struggle of developing genuine skills.

**Electronic wallpaper** (Dave Grimmer, Denso) — Data dashboards and displays with no corresponding action taken. The failure mode of Industry 4.0 deployment without TPS thinking.

**Enabling bureaucracy** (Paul Adler) — Standards, rules, and audits used to enable and develop people rather than control and punish them. Toyota's organizational archetype. Combines mechanistic structure with organic social enabling.

**Fast PDCA** (Denso) — Complete a plan-do-check-act cycle within a single shift using real-time IoT data, rather than over weeks.

**5S (Seiri, Seiton, Seiso, Seiketsu, Shitsuke)** — Sort, Straighten, Shine, Standardize, Sustain. Systematic workplace organization for visual management and waste exposure.

**Gemba / Genba** — "The real place." Where value-added work actually happens. The production floor, the repair shop, the customer site. Go there; don't manage from reports.

**Genchi genbutsu** — "The actual place and actual thing." Go directly to the source to find facts. Make correct decisions based on observed reality. Make decisions in the hands of those closest to the problem.

**Growth mindset** (Carol Dweck, cited) — Belief that individuals can develop through effort throughout life. Toyota hires for this potential. The organizational analog is kaizen culture.

**Han** — Japanese elementary school group of 4–6 students; children learn to work "as" a group (collective identity, roles, listening, delegation) not just "in" a group. A cultural root of Toyota teamwork.

**Hansei** — "Being honest about your own weaknesses." Process of reflection to recognize one's mistakes, feel sincerely concerned about them, and take appropriate steps to avoid recurrence. A mindset and an attitude, not just a meeting format.

**Heijunka** — "Leveling." Levels production demand by type and quantity over a fixed period to create smooth flow, reducing mura (unevenness) and muri (overburden). Foundation for flow, pull, and standardized work.

**Hoshin kanri** — "Policy deployment." Toyota's annual system for aligning goals, plans, and improvement efforts at all levels through planning + execution PDCA. Also a process for developing people through coaching.

**Hourensou (hou/ren/sou)** — "Report-inform-consult." Daily communication discipline: "hou" (report) + "ren" (periodic updates) + "sou" (consult/advise). Enables just-in-time coaching and cross-functional information sharing.

**Jidoka** — Machine with intelligence to stop itself whenever a problem is detected. Frees operator for value-added work and problem solving. "Automation with a human touch." Second pillar of the TPS house.

**Jishuken** — "Voluntary study groups." Supplier TPS learning groups doing radical transformation projects together; 3–4 month focused interventions.

**JIT (Just-in-time)** — System of continuous flow delivering materials and information in small lots to the point of use as needed — neither too early nor too late. Eliminates overproduction, quickly surfaces abnormalities. First pillar of the TPS house. Attributed to Kiichiro Toyoda.

**K4 (kozokeikaku)** — Structure plan study drawings circulated to 100+ stakeholders in vehicle development. Embodies nemawashi and set-based thinking. Toyota's primary tool for building consensus in product development.

**Kaizen** — "Change for the better" / "continuous improvement." Both a philosophy of striving for excellence and a method of iterative improvement following PDCA. Engages the entire organization. At Toyota, it is the daily scientific way people work toward goals — not a special event.

**Kanban** — Scheduling signal for JIT production. A binary signal (card, empty bin, sound, lights, or electronics) authorizing replenishment of exactly what is needed. Puts the customer in control of initiating production. Two types: withdrawal kanban (authorize movement) and production kanban (authorize production).

**Kata** — Two meanings: (1) form/way of doing things; (2) pattern of movements practiced to develop fundamental skill. Applied to both improvement kata (4-step) and coaching kata (5-question).

**Keiretsu** — Post-WWII Japanese groupings of integrated enterprises; basis for close long-term business relationships.

**Key kanban** — A mechanical, non-electric mistake-proofing device: a color-coded metal kanban acts as a physical key opening only the correct parts bin, simultaneously serving as replenishment signal and poka-yoke.

**Lean** — Term coined by John Krafcik at MIT, popularized by Womack/Jones/Roos. Describes TPS: uses less of everything vs. mass production (half human effort, space, tools, engineering hours, far less inventory, fewer defects, greater variety). Toyota itself prefers "TPS" or "Toyota Way" — lean is the outside world's name.

**Muda** — Waste: anything that takes time but does not add value for the customer. **Seven types**: overproduction, waiting, unnecessary transport, overprocessing, excess inventory, unnecessary movement, defects. **Overproduction is the fundamental waste** — it causes all the others.

**Mura** — Unevenness; irregular production schedule or fluctuating volumes; forces holding maximum capacity even when average is lower; causes the bullwhip effect through supply chain.

**Muri** — Overburdening people or equipment beyond natural limits; causes safety/quality problems and breakdowns; can cause muda.

**Nemawashi** — "Going around the roots." Informal process of laying the foundation for a proposed change by talking to all stakeholders, gathering input, building consensus before formal announcement.

**Obeya** — "Big room." Large visual management and planning room used for collaborative planning, strategy execution, and daily management meetings. Used in vehicle development (chief engineer system), hoshin kanri, and daily management.

**OMCD (Operations Management Consulting Division)** — Elite TPS expert corps started by Ohno in 1968: ~6 senior TPS gurus + ~50 consultants; coaches Toyota plants and key suppliers. US equivalent: TSSC.

**OPR (Operational Ratio)** — Toyota expects ~97% of scheduled vehicles built on time during planned hours. Achieved not by preventing problems but by solving them continuously. The paradox: intentionally fragile systems + relentless problem solving = high reliability.

**PDCA** — Plan-Do-Check-Act. Cornerstone of scientific thinking and continuous improvement. The true version navigates with a compass under uncertainty; the false version implements assumed solutions.

**Pilot team** — When new product launches, workers from major factory areas brought together full-time to critique design for manufacturability, develop initial standardized work, then turn over to production teams. 3-year assignment.

**Poka-yoke** — Error proofing / mistake proofing. Creative devices making errors nearly impossible. Each has its own standard maintenance form.

**Power meeting** — Toyota's daily visual management standup meeting at the group leader level in front of KPI boards. Brief; action-focused; standing.

**Pull system** — Production triggered by actual demand signal; customer pulls from upstream. Avoids overproduction. After materials are used, a signal authorizes replacement.

**SDCA (Standard-Do-Check-Act)** — Deviation management at the frontline. For deviations from existing standards. A problem is a deviation from standard. Complement to PDCA.

**Sensei** — Honored teacher of TPS who has achieved mastery repeatedly demonstrated at the gemba. Not a consultant or a speaker — someone who has done it and teaches through challenge and observation.

**Set-based design** — Starting with a broad set of design alternatives and systematically narrowing rather than committing to a single concept early. Avoids commitment to an inferior plan requiring wasteful rework iterations.

**SMED (Single-Minute Exchange of Dies)** — Shigeo Shingo's method for rapid changeover. Internal setup (machine stopped) vs. external setup (machine running). Move everything possible to external; then minimize internal.

**Standardized work** — The current best-known way of performing work at the rate of customer demand. Forms the baseline for kaizen. As standards improve, new standard becomes the baseline for further improvement. "Today's best-known way, which can be improved."

**Takt time (takt)** — German for "rhythm/meter." Rate of customer demand: available work time ÷ units required. Every step should produce at this rate. Going faster = overproduction. Going slower = bottleneck. The fundamental pacing mechanism of JIT.

**Target condition** — Next level of performance to strive toward; defines the gap for the next PDCA cycle. Specific, time-bound, includes both outcome metric and process metric. Set one at a time — do not plan beyond your threshold of knowledge.

**TBP (Toyota Business Practices)** — Fujio Cho's 8-step problem-solving process for developing Toyota Way thinking through practice. Not a rigid method; a developmental framework for building scientific thinkers.

**Threshold of knowledge** — The boundary of what is currently understood. Each experiment extends this threshold. Do not plan beyond it. The argument against detailed multi-year implementation plans.

**TPS (Toyota Production System)** — Toyota's unique manufacturing approach; basis for lean production. Constructed in conditions of low demand and high variety. Based on jidoka and JIT. Produces high-quality products one at a time, on demand. Toyota itself now calls it a "Thinking Production System."

**TPS Basic Learning Line** — A manual transmission assembly line (no electricity) used by Mitsuru Kawai for deep training in TPS fundamentals. Students improve the manual line, then apply insights to automated processes. Learning must be earned, not assumed.

**TSSC (Toyota Production System Support Center)** — Originally Toyota Supplier Support Center (1992). Teaches TPS to suppliers and non-Toyota organizations using model-line transformation approach. Served 320+ organizations in 25 years; average results: 75% inventory reduction, 124% productivity improvement.

**TWI (Training Within Industry)** — US WWII program (1940–1945) that heavily influenced Toyota: job methods, job instruction, job relations, program development. The Job Instruction Training method is still used at Toyota.

**Value stream** — The complete set of activities (value-added and waste) to transform input into output the customer wants.

**Value stream mapping** — Method to understand material and information flow in work processes. Current-state map shows how value flows and the wastes blocking it. Future-state map is an aspirational vision.

**Variable workforce** — Temporary workers employed through an outside agency (up to 2 years) providing a buffer to protect regular workforce job security during demand fluctuations.

**Visual control** — Any communication device in the work environment that tells at a glance how work should be done and whether it is deviating from the standard.

**Yamazumi chart** — Work-balance chart breaking down work elements per station, showing cycle times vs. takt. Used to identify bottlenecks and rebalance workload.

**Yokoten** — "Across everywhere" / "spread horizontally." Horizontal, peer-to-peer transfer of lean knowledge. Not "copy exactly" — people go see for themselves, reflect, and creatively adapt knowledge to their local problems.

# PART 5: THE TPS PROBLEM-SOLVING MINDSET

## How Toyota Thinkers Approach Problems Differently

### The Fundamental Mindset Contrast

| Standard Organization | Toyota |
|---|---|
| Problems are failures → hide them, blame someone, fix quietly | Problems are **learning opportunities** → surface them, stop to fix at source, learn |
| Inventory and buffers are safety nets → comfort | Inventory is waste and a hiding place for problems → remove buffers to expose problems |
| Run everything as hard and fast as possible → productivity | Run at the rate of customer demand → overproduction is the fundamental waste |
| Experts design the best way → workers execute | Workers closest to the work are the best industrial engineers → they design and improve their own standards |
| Buy technology to solve problems | People and processes first; technology only to support people |
| Optimize each department independently | Optimize the entire value stream → local optimization often suboptimizes the system |
| Quarterly profit is the primary goal | Long-term purpose and survival is the primary goal → profits are a result, not the mission |
| Certainty is sought; uncertainty is failure | Certainty is impossible; uncertainty is the starting condition for all problem solving |

### The "Problems Are the System Working Correctly" Insight

Toyota doesn't just tolerate problems — it engineers systems to *create* problems (by removing buffers, enforcing one-piece flow, connecting processes tightly) so that problems surface immediately, are solved by the people closest to them, and never happen again.

Every andon pull, every line stop, every missing kanban, every deviation from standardized work is the TPS system performing its designed function: making the invisible visible so it can be fixed forever.

This is why Cho told Scaffede that **never stopping the line was bad news** — it meant problems were hidden. This is why Ohno removed inventory (lowered the water level) to expose rocks. This is why one-piece flow was intentionally described as "fragile" (Krafcik) — fragility is a feature, not a bug.

### Counterintuitive TPS Truths (How Toyota Thinks Differently From Mass Production)

1. Often best to **idle a machine** to avoid overproduction (the fundamental waste)
2. Often best to **build inventory of finished goods** to level the production schedule (heijunka requires some strategic inventory)
3. Often best to **selectively add overhead** to support value-added workers (like supporting surgeons)
4. It's NOT always best to keep workers making parts as fast as possible — produce at the rate of customer demand
5. **Automation is not always better** than manual — people are the most flexible resource
6. **Planning slowly and carefully**, then experimenting, then deploying efficiently, is often faster than rushing
7. **Stopping production to fix problems** improves productivity — because you eliminate downstream waste, rework, and defect escapes
8. To increase throughput, analysts/workers may need to work on **fewer** projects at a time (WIP cap paradox)

### The TPS Problem-Solving Stack (How Problems Are Actually Handled at Toyota)

**Level 1 — Immediate response (seconds to minutes)**:
- Team member sees deviation from standard → pulls andon cord
- Team leader responds within 10-20 seconds
- Assess: safety issue? quality issue? out of standard?
- Contain: fix immediately, or tag and set aside with clear marking
- Stop line segment if needed; rarely the entire plant

**Level 2 — Same shift / same day**:
- Team leader examines andon pulls → selects most frequent or severe
- First question: Was standardized work followed?
  - If no → train, correct behavior
  - If yes + still defects → standardized work must change
- Countermeasure applied by tomorrow morning (Ohno's rule: "no problem should wait longer than tomorrow morning")

**Level 3 — Systematic root cause analysis (days to weeks)**:
- 5 Whys: drill from symptom to root cause
- Pareto charts: prioritize by frequency/impact
- Cause-and-effect diagrams: map possible causes
- Genchi genbutsu: go to the actual site, observe actual conditions, handle actual objects
- A3 report: structured problem-solving document (one page, visualizes the story)

**Level 4 — PDCA / Kaizen (weeks to months)**:
- Plan: define target condition, identify gap, hypothesize countermeasure
- Do: run experiment in limited scope
- Check: compare actual vs. predicted results; what did we learn?
- Act: if successful → standardize and spread via yokoten; if not → adjust and rerun

**Level 5 — Strategic problem solving (months to years)**:
- Senior leaders define challenging long-term goals
- Goals cascaded through all levels via hoshin kanri and catchball
- Each level creates their own plans aligned to organizational goals
- Measured with leading indicators (sub-KPIs), not just lagging financial results
- Execution = mountain climbing through PDCA cycles navigating obstacles

### "Go and See" as an Epistemological Practice

"Going to the gemba" is not about physical location — it is about the epistemological commitment to ground decisions in observed reality rather than assumption.

**Ohno's 1973 TPS manual**: "On the shop floor it is important to start with the actual phenomenon and search for the root cause in order to solve the problem. We must emphasize 'getting the facts.'"

**The conference-room failure mode**: When intelligent people analyze problems in conference rooms without going to see, they fabricate root causes. The blue baby hospital case demonstrates this perfectly — the team "knew" the cause before going to look and was wrong. Every wrong countermeasure wastes resources and builds cynicism about problem solving.

**The Ohba formulation**: "TPS is built on the scientific way of thinking... How do I respond to this problem? Not a toolbox. [You have to be] willing to start small, learn through trial and error."

# PART 6: DEVELOPING PROBLEM SOLVERS

## The Sensei/Student Dynamic

### What a Toyota Sensei Does

A Toyota sensei is not a consultant, lecturer, or expert who provides answers. The sensei:

- **Goes to the gemba** and observes without speaking — then asks challenging questions
- **Does NOT give the answer** — creates the conditions for the student to discover it
- **Pushes back on A3s** — marks them up with red ink, sends them back, makes the student's weak thinking visible
- **Sets impossible-seeming challenges** — forces breakdown of current thinking and creative experimentation
- **Creates discomfort deliberately** — "Try something and do it now" (Ohno)
- **Explains the criticism after the fact** — not during it. Ohno: "He would gather us at 7 pm and explain why he was critical that day. Everyone appreciated he was trying to teach them."

### The Ohno Circle as the Archetypal Teaching Method

Ohno drew a circle on the shop floor and told his student to stand in it and observe — for hours, sometimes all day, without explanation.

**What this teaches**:
- The discipline of sustained observation without preconceptions
- That the gemba contains more information than any report
- That the student must figure out what to look for — the sensei won't tell them
- That thinking cannot be rushed
- That you cannot improve what you haven't deeply observed

This single technique encapsulates the entire Toyota approach to developing problem solvers: create the conditions for struggle, then coach the reflection.

### The Dallis Training Sequence (from Steven Spear's research into Toyota's production system)

1. Observe work processes → identify small problems → make changes → evaluate changes (6-week cycles)
2. Weekly mentor check: What did you observe? What were the problems? What changes did you make? What was the expected impact?
3. Friday review: compare expectations vs. results
4. Sent to Japan for intensive kaizen (target: 50 changes in 3 days = 1 change every 22 minutes)
5. Return to US to close remaining gaps (achieved 99% availability vs. initial 90%)

**Four lessons from this training**:
1. No substitute for direct observation
2. Proposed changes must always be structured as experiments
3. Workers and managers experiment as frequently as possible
4. Managers coach, not fix

### How Toyota Builds Problem-Solving Capability Over Time

**At TMUK (Toyota Motor Manufacturing UK)** — formal TBP career path:
- Formal TBP project at each level of career advancement, every 3–4 years
- Report-out on a strict 15-minute A3 to a panel of three; pass/fail with "ready to mentor" rating
- Third phase: OJD (On-the-Job Development) — trains the coach by having them coach a TBP project while being coached by a "second coach" (meta-coaching)

**At Toyota Georgetown** — quality circles:
- Every team member participates in 6-step problem solving in their work group
- Led by team leader; group leader coaches
- Best projects presented regionally and globally; winners to Japan
- 80,000 suggestions per year; 99% implemented

**The 8-year cascade at one plant**: TBP started at senior executive level with Fujio Cho as examiner. Cascaded down the organization. Each level coaches the next. Required 8 years to reach group leader level. This timeline is not a failure — it is evidence of how seriously Toyota takes genuine capability development.

### The Herman Miller Model (Non-Toyota Application)

**Bridge Program** (production facilitators — equivalent of team leader):
- 12-week intense training
- Weeks of gemba work, current-condition building, problem solving through PDCA, standardized work practice
- Followed by 3-month internship

**Propel Program** (work team leaders):
- Includes writing monthly A3 plans
- Developing cadence for management
- Charting material and information flow
- Toyota Business Practices 8-step problem solving
- 3-month internship

Jill Miller (Herman Miller): "Bridge not only gives you the skills, tools, and proficiency in those tools to lead a business but also grows you in a moral and logical way."

### How Problem-Solving Capability Is Built: The Neurological Case

Scientific thinking must be practiced until it becomes habitual. The neurological basis: repeated practice of scientific-thinking behaviors creates neural pathways. With enough repetition, slow thinking (System 2) becomes more natural — the neural path becomes the path of least resistance.

**Starter kata as training wheels**: Toyota Kata's "starter kata" are simple practice routines designed to build neural pathways for scientific thinking. They are not the destination — they are scaffolding to be gradually internalized until the scientific thinking pattern operates automatically.

**The Dunning-Kruger danger**: Beginners overestimate their ability. The person who reads about 5 Whys and thinks they can do it is the most dangerous person in a lean transformation — they skip the struggle that builds genuine capability.

### The Coaching Kata as Developmental Infrastructure

The Coaching Kata's 5 questions are not just coaching questions — they are the architecture of a scientific-thinking conversation:

1. **What is the target condition?** → Forces clarity about the goal
2. **What is the actual condition now?** → Forces fact-based current-state understanding
3. **What obstacles are preventing you from reaching the target condition? Which one are you addressing now?** → Forces obstacle identification and prioritization
4. **What is your next step (experiment)? What do you expect?** → Forces the learner to make a prediction (hypothesis)
5. **How quickly can we go and see what we have learned from taking that step?** → Forces experimentation and learning orientation

Used consistently, this conversation structure teaches scientific thinking regardless of the content of the conversation.

# PART 7: VISUAL MANAGEMENT & STANDARDS

## The Purpose of Visual Management

Visual management is not about aesthetics or organization for its own sake. **The purpose is to make problems immediately visible to anyone — before they require an expert to detect them.**

The underlying biological principle: "Hear a piece of information, and three days later you'll remember 10% of it. Add a picture and you'll remember 65%." (John Medina)

## Standards as the Foundation for Both Visual Control and Kaizen

The relationship:
```
Standard → Deviation visible → Problem identified → Root cause found → Countermeasure →
New Standard → Deviation visible at new level → Next problem identified → ...
```

Without a standard, there is no deviation. Without a deviation, there is no problem. Without a problem, there is no kaizen. Without kaizen, standards do not improve. Standards and kaizen are two sides of the same coin.

**First question when any defect is found**: "Was standardized work followed?"
- If no → correct behavior (training problem or compliance problem)
- If yes and defects still occur → modify standardized work (standard is wrong)

This question determines whether you have a people problem or a system problem. Most organizations default to "people problem." Toyota defaults to "system problem."

## Key Visual Management Tools and Their Problem-Solving Function

**Standardized Worksheet** (posted outward, facing the manager):
- Shows takt time, cycle time, walk pattern, work sequence, quality checkpoints
- Allows the manager to audit compliance visually at a glance
- Deviations from the standard are immediately visible
- Walk time that is 40% of job = obvious waste target visible to everyone

**Process Control Board**:
- Date/time columns in 15-minute increments
- Planned vs. actual batches completed
- Color magnets: green = on time, red = behind
- Supervisor fills in each morning before team arrives
- Every 15 minutes: anyone can see status of every associate vs. plan
- Enables immediate resource redeployment before falling further behind

**Andon Display Board**:
- Lights blinking green (normal), yellow (attention needed), red (stopped)
- Real-time visibility of process status across the entire floor
- Makes the invisible (problems) visible without walking the entire floor

**Shadow Board**:
- Outline/shadow of each tool in its proper place
- Missing tool is immediately visible without searching
- The shadow IS the standard; deviation from the shadow IS the problem

**Obeya** (in product development and hoshin):
- All dimensions of plan status visible in one large room
- Schedule status by area vs. plan, quality data, financial status, competitor benchmarks
- Any deviation from schedule or targets immediately visible
- Enables fast/accurate decision-making; creates team integration

**KPI Boards** (FMDS standard):
- Safety/Environment → Quality → Productivity → Cost → HR Development
- Main KPIs (outcomes) + Sub-KPIs (causal factors) + Focus items (actions)
- Daily standups in front of these boards
- Visual connection between daily actions and strategic hoshin goals

## The Danger of "Electronic Wallpaper"

Toyota's principle: if the information doesn't prompt action and learning, it has no value regardless of how impressive the dashboard looks.

Denso's lesson with IoT: The initial deployment created beautiful dashboards that nobody acted on. The discipline of TPS thinking — what does this data mean? what is the countermeasure? — had to be explicitly developed. Technology accelerates learning only when the people using it have scientific thinking capability.

# PART 8: LEADERSHIP BEHAVIORS

## What Toyota Leaders Do Differently

### The Primary Job of Leaders: Develop People

Toyota leaders measure their success by the scientific thinking capability and problem-solving skill of their direct reports — not by the problems they personally solve.

**"Before we build cars, we build people."**

**Fujio Cho on Ohno's leadership**: "Mr. Ohno could be very critical when he saw something wrong. It was very difficult. But then at the end of the day he would explain why he was critical. Everyone, even the junior workers, appreciated that he was trying to teach them."

This is the model: create challenge, allow struggle, then teach the thinking — not during the struggle but after. Giving the answer during the struggle short-circuits the learning.

### Leaders Go to the Gemba — Specifically and Purposefully

Akio Toyoda (2009): "In a speech I made shortly after becoming president in 2009 I vowed to be closest to the gemba. Whenever there are real objects there is a gemba. The only way we can really understand the problems is at the gemba."

**What distinguishes Toyota gemba walks from superficial "walks"**:
- Goes to the gemba with specific questions and hypotheses to test
- Observes the actual process; looks for deviations from standardized work
- Asks team members to explain what they're doing and why — not to check compliance but to surface understanding
- Identifies problems the data hasn't surfaced yet
- Coaches, does not inspect and direct

**Mike Masaki (Toyota Technical Center president)**: Conducted twice-yearly desk/file cabinet audits of every employee — then expanded to email organization systems. The signal sent: visual control is fundamental at every level, not optional.

### Leader Standard Work

What Toyota leaders do daily — the pattern of leadership behaviors expected at the gemba:

- **Power meeting**: Daily standup in front of KPI boards with direct reports; 15-30 minutes; action-focused
- **Gemba observation**: Regular unscheduled walks to observe actual conditions, not to receive briefings
- **A3 coaching**: Review and mark up direct reports' A3s; send back with questions, not corrections
- **Andon response** (team leaders): Available within seconds when andon is pulled; owns the response
- **Process audit**: Can anyone walk through and recognize if standardized work is being followed?
- **Safety and quality first**: Every meeting begins with a personal safety commitment; all leaders receive safety incidents on their phone instantly

### Leaders Do Not Provide Answers

The most important and most difficult behavior: resisting the impulse to give the answer.

**The Coaching Kata's 5 questions** are a concrete behavioral substitute for answer-giving. Instead of saying "here's what you should do," ask: "What is your target condition? What's the actual condition now? What obstacles are you addressing? What's your next experiment? When can we see results?"

**Gary Convis**: "I got where I am because of trial and error and failure and perseverance. That trial and error was on the floor under the direction of my Japanese mentors."

### Leaders Forgive Sound Process, Reprimand Lucky Shortcuts

"Management will forgive a decision that does not work out as expected if the process used was a good one. A decision that by chance works out well, but was based on a shortcut process, is more likely to lead to a reprimand." — Toyota management ethos

This single behavioral norm creates the conditions for genuine scientific thinking. If people are rewarded for quick results regardless of method, they will always shortcut. If they are rewarded for rigorous process, they will invest in scientific thinking.

### Leaders Create Job Security as the Foundation of Kaizen

"The greatest sin under Ohno was to take things for granted and assume you know."

But team members will only surface problems — will only surface their own knowledge gaps — when they trust that doing so will not threaten their employment.

Toyota's job security is not charity — it is a business strategy. The knowledge, skills, and problem-solving capability of long-tenured, secure team members cannot be purchased in the market. It can only be grown. Protecting that capability is protecting the competitive advantage.

### Yamashina's 10 Management Principles (Selected)

Principle 3: "Think and speak based on verified, proven information and data. Go and confirm facts for yourself. You are responsible for the information you report."

Principle 4: "Take full advantage of wisdom and experiences of others — send, gather, or discuss information."

# PART 9: COMMON PITFALLS

## What Non-Toyota Organizations Get Wrong

### Pitfall 1: Treating TPS as a Toolkit

The most pervasive mistake. Organizations implement 5S, kanban, and kaizen events as discrete projects. Each tool produces some local improvement. No learning occurs. No change in thinking. Tools wither after the project team leaves.

**Ohno's warning**: "If we write it down, we kill it." TPS is a living system of thinking. Tools are expressions of that thinking. You cannot separate the tool from the thinking without getting an empty form.

**The nuclear energy VP story**: VP showed Toyota sensei his lean assessment system tied to plant manager bonuses and enterprise-wide lean deployment. Sensei said: "Please stop doing that." Start ONE model line. VP never stopped. Brought in Liker despite the warning. Result: waste of time. Lesson: You cannot spread what nobody truly understands yet.

### Pitfall 2: Using False PDCA

Plan = commit to a known solution → Do = implement → Check = confirm it worked → Act = broadly deploy. This is project management disguised as scientific thinking.

The tell: when the "Check" step is used only to confirm success (not to learn from predictions that didn't match reality), the organization is using PDCA as theater. Real PDCA treats a failed prediction as the most valuable outcome of the cycle.

### Pitfall 3: 5 Whys Without Genchi Genbutsu

Asking "Why?" five times in a conference room, without going to the gemba to observe the actual phenomenon, consistently produces plausible-sounding false root causes. The result: a countermeasure that doesn't work, and cynicism about root cause analysis.

The blue baby hospital case illustrates this precisely: the entire intelligent team in the conference room "knew" the cause before going to look — and was wrong on every count.

### Pitfall 4: Implementing Standards Without Engaging Workers

Taylorist standardization — experts design the one best way, supervisors enforce, workers comply — is the opposite of Toyota. It produces resistance, workarounds, and static procedures.

Toyota's enabling bureaucracy works because **workers design and own their own standards**. The stopwatch is given to the work group. The standardized worksheet is developed by the people doing the work, not the industrial engineering department.

### Pitfall 5: Kaizen Events as a Substitute for Daily Kaizen

Three-day or five-day kaizen workshops (kaizen blitzes) produce burst improvement followed by gradual regression. This is because the daily discipline — standardized work, visual management, PDCA, team leader involvement — doesn't exist to sustain the gains.

Toyota's kaizen is not an event. It is the daily scientific way people work toward goals. The event is only a tool for initial learning; the discipline of daily kaizen is the real mechanism.

### Pitfall 6: Visual Management Without Stable Processes

"5S is lipstick on a pig" when applied to a mass production system without stable processes. Organizing waste neatly does not eliminate waste. The Donnelly Mirrors story: a Ford Taurus literally disappeared into inventory piles for months — they had done 5S on top of a system that had no standard inventory locations or flow.

5S should support smooth flow to takt. It has no value in a system that doesn't know what "standard" looks like.

### Pitfall 7: Technology Before Process Understanding

"Americans tend to think that buying expensive new technology is a good way to solve problems. Toyota prefers to first use people and processes to solve problems, then supplement and support its people with technology later."

Every technology investment Toyota makes begins with: does a stable, well-understood manual process already exist that this technology would support? If not — first work out the process, then automate.

The TPS Basic Learning Line (no electricity, manual) is the deliberate counterpoint to automation culture: you must first understand the process at its most fundamental level.

### Pitfall 8: Hoshin Kanri as Target Cascading

Many organizations cascade targets down the hierarchy (each level gets a portion of the corporate target) and call it policy deployment. This misses everything that makes hoshin effective:

- There is no **catchball** — no genuine dialogue between levels
- There is no **causal reasoning** — targets are split proportionally, not logically
- Leaders manage results from the boardroom, not from the gemba (sherpa vs. boardroom observer)
- There is no **hansei** — no sincere reflection on why gaps occurred
- There is no **yokoten** — successful problem solutions are not spread horizontally

### Pitfall 9: Importing Leaders Instead of Developing Them

Toyota grows leaders from within who thoroughly understand the actual work. Leaders who come from outside and have not done the work cannot coach what they haven't practiced. They will manage by metrics, not by observation. They will give answers instead of asking questions.

Gary Convis (first non-Japanese president of a Toyota plant): "I got where I am because of trial and error and failure and perseverance. That trial and error was on the floor under the direction of my Japanese mentors." He was developed over decades at Toyota, not hired in.

### Pitfall 10: Mistaking Compliance for Capability

A team that follows the standard is not the same as a team that owns the standard. A team that uses the A3 template is not the same as a team that thinks scientifically. A team that pulls the andon is not the same as a team that solves root causes.

The test of genuine TPS capability: when the sensei or the consultant leaves, does the improvement continue? In most organizations, it doesn't — because compliance was built, not capability. At Toyota, the practice is sustained because the scientific thinking is internalized as the natural way to work.

### Pitfall 11: Solving Problems in Conference Rooms

"The greatest sin under Ohno was to take things for granted and assume you know." The response to any problem, in any domain, must begin with going to observe the actual phenomenon in the actual place.

Problems analyzed without genchi genbutsu consistently result in:
- Countermeasures that address symptoms, not causes
- Multiple iterations of "solution" → recurrence → new "solution" → recurrence
- Growing cynicism about problem-solving methods
- People learning to present plausible-sounding analyses rather than factual ones

### Pitfall 12: Stopping the Lean Transformation When Tools Are in Place

Many organizations declare "we've implemented lean" when kanban is running, 5S is visible, and kaizen events are scheduled. This is the beginning, not the end.

The retired lean sensei's answer when asked "are we world class?": "I do not know. I was not here yesterday." You can only judge whether improvement is happening day to day, not state at a point in time.

The TPS house is never finished. The foundation is always being reinforced. The pillars are always being strengthened. The goal — highest quality, lowest cost, shortest lead time, best safety, high morale — is a direction, not a destination.
