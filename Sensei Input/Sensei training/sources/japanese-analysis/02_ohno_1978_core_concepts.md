# Ohno 1978 (『トヨタ生産方式 ― 脱規模の経営をめざして』) — Core Concepts with Nuance Analysis

**Source book:** 大野耐一『トヨタ生産方式 ― 脱規模の経営をめざして』ダイヤモンド社, 1978. ISBN 978-4-478-46001-6. English: *Toyota Production System: Beyond Large-Scale Production* (Productivity Press, 1988).

**How this file works:** I am not reproducing the book. For each concept I give (a) the Japanese term(s) with the kanji Ohno uses, (b) short public-domain phrasings widely quoted in Japanese secondary sources, (c) my own English translation, (d) what the English editions lose, and (e) page pointers so you can verify in your English copy and consult the Japanese edition if you purchase it.

## 1. 自働化 (jidōka) — *autonomation with a human touch*

**This is the single most important nuance gap in all of TPS.**

### 原文 (term and description)

The character Ohno uses is **自働化** — with 働 (*hataraku*, "to work," containing the *ninben* 亻, the "person" radical) — *not* 自動化 (ordinary automation, with 動 "to move").

Ohno inherited the concept from Sakichi Toyoda's (豊田佐吉) G型自動織機 (Type-G loom, 1924) — which stopped itself the instant a thread broke. The loom's patent records use 自動; Ohno deliberately re-wrote the compound as 自働 (with ninben) in his TPS writings to make explicit what Sakichi's machine *did* that ordinary automation doesn't. In Ohno's framing:

> 「ニンベンの付いた自働化」 ≒ "Automation with a human touch / *autonomation*"

Widely phrased in Japanese teaching materials as: *機械に人間の知恵を持たせる* ("to endow the machine with human wisdom").

### 英訳

The canonical English is "Autonomation" or "Automation with a human touch." Both are accurate but neither is intuitive. Most English readers see "automation" and mentally round back to 自動化.

### ニュアンス・ギャップ分析

- **The kanji itself is the argument.** By inserting 亻 (the person radical) into the middle character, Ohno is saying: *a machine that truly works is one that contains a trace of human intelligence — specifically, the intelligence to stop itself when something is wrong.* The character is a mnemonic. No English word can carry this.
- Sakichi's 自働織機 stopped on a broken thread. Jidōka is the generalization: **any machine that halts itself on abnormality is "working" in the full human sense; any machine that just runs is merely "moving."**
- The English "autonomation" is a neologism that tries to carry this but sounds like jargon. "Automation with a human touch" sounds soft. The Japanese is sharp: *real work = work that can detect its own failure and stop*.
- **Operational consequence in the English-speaking world:** many lean adopters implement "automation" without jidōka's halt-on-abnormality principle, and then wonder why they don't get Toyota's quality. The kanji is warning you. The English isn't.

### 出典

## 2. ジャスト・イン・タイム (Just In Time) / JIT

### 原文

Ohno's canonical phrasing, now embedded in every Japanese manufacturing textbook:

> **「必要なものを、必要なときに、必要なだけ」**
> (*hitsuyō na mono wo, hitsuyō na toki ni, hitsuyō na dake*)

### 英訳

Commonly rendered in English as: "the right things, at the right time, in the right quantity." The more literal and faithful translation:

> "What is needed, when it is needed, only as much as is needed."

### ニュアンス・ギャップ分析

- The Japanese is built on the repetition of 必要 (*hitsuyō*, "necessary") three times. It is a drumbeat. The English "right" is a softer, more flexible word. 必要 is sharper — it implies a *need* that is real and bounded, not a subjective "right-ness."
- 〜だけ (*dake*, "only / just") is an exclusionary particle. It means *nothing beyond that*. The English "in the right quantity" can imply "the correct amount," which could be any amount; the Japanese insists *only that amount and no more*. This is why 在庫のムダ (inventory waste) sits so hard in Japanese Toyota thinking: *any more than necessary is, definitionally, waste.*
- The phrase's rhythm in Japanese makes it memorable to workers on the floor. It's a mantra. The English version is explanatory; it's not a mantra you'd chant.

### Origin

Coined by Kiichiro Toyoda (豊田喜一郎) in 1938 when he founded the automotive operation; operationalized by Ohno starting in the 1950s. The concept's surface analogue is the American supermarket (Ohno visited US supermarkets in 1956 and saw pull-based replenishment).

## 3. ムリ・ムラ・ムダ (Muri / Mura / Muda) — the "3 M's"

### 原文

**ムリ・ムラ・ムダ** — three katakana words, in this order, as used inside Toyota Group.

- **ムリ** (*muri*) — overburden, unreasonableness, pushing beyond capacity
- **ムラ** (*mura*) — unevenness, variability, irregularity
- **ムダ** (*muda*) — waste, non-value-add

Toyota's internal teaching phrase:

> 「ムリをかければムラが生じ、ひいてはムダの発生原因になる」
> (*muri wo kakereba mura ga shōjī, hiite wa muda no hassei gen'in ni naru*)

### 英訳

> "If you impose overburden, unevenness arises, and in turn that becomes the origin of waste."

### ニュアンス・ギャップ分析

- **The order matters.** Most English lean literature writes them as "Muda, Mura, Muri" (waste first) because muda is the concept that grabs Western management attention. Toyota's internal phrasing is the **reverse**: Muri → Mura → Muda, because that's the *causal chain*. Waste is a symptom; overburden is the cause.
- Inverting the order changes the mental model. In "Muda-Mura-Muri," waste is the primary object of attention. In "Muri-Mura-Muda," waste is where you notice the disease, but you treat it at the source (Muri).
- **ムリ** is almost always weakly translated as "overburden" but also means *unreasonable*, *impossible*, *straining*. It applies equally to machines, people, and process design. English lean culture has tended to apply it mainly to operator fatigue; Japanese applies it to everything, including asking a process to do something it isn't designed for.
- **ムラ** is commonly translated as "unevenness" but includes quality variance, volume variance, work-content variance, and time-interval variance. It's the *variance-to-any-mean* concept. 標準 (*hyōjun*, "standard") is the opposite of mura; this is why standardized work is TPS's answer to mura.
- **ムダ** literally means *useless / in vain* (ムダな = useless). The 7 wastes are all species of ムダ.

### Informal Japanese mnemonic

In Japanese shop-floor training, the 3 M's are sometimes collapsed into the portmanteau **ダラリ** (dara-ri), from **ム**ダ・**ム**ラ・**ム**リ rotated as "だ・ら・り" — a kind of slouching/unproductive sound. Being "ダラリ" is bad.

## 4. 七つのムダ (the Seven Wastes)

### 原文

Ohno's canonical list of 7 ムダ (in the order Ohno presents them, which subtly matters):

1. **つくりすぎのムダ** (*tsukurisugi no muda*) — overproduction
2. **手待ちのムダ** (*temachi no muda*) — waiting
3. **運搬のムダ** (*unpan no muda*) — transport/conveyance
4. **加工そのもののムダ** (*kakō sonomono no muda*) — over-processing (literally *processing itself*)
5. **在庫のムダ** (*zaiko no muda*) — inventory
6. **動作のムダ** (*dōsa no muda*) — (wasted) motion
7. **不良をつくるムダ** (*furyō wo tsukuru muda*) — defects (literally *making defectives*)

### ニュアンス・ギャップ分析

- **つくりすぎのムダ is #1 and is the worst.** Ohno considered overproduction the **parent of all other wastes** — it hides the other six. If you don't overproduce, you expose inventory waste, transport waste, motion waste, waiting, etc. The English lists often present the 7 as a flat list; the Japanese ordering encodes a hierarchy.
- **加工そのもののムダ** (the waste of processing itself) is often translated as "over-processing." The Japanese nuance is sharper: it asks *is this processing step itself waste?* — not "are we doing too much of it?" but *is this step necessary at all?* It's a challenge to the step's existence, not its dosage.
- **不良をつくるムダ** — the kanji 不良をつくる means *to make defectives*. English says "defects." The Japanese points at the *act of producing them*, which places responsibility on the process. "Defects" sounds like an outcome; 不良をつくる sounds like an action you are doing.
- **動作のムダ** — *motion* waste, not *movement* — 動作 specifically means the bodily motions of the worker. It's about micro-ergonomics. English "motion" can be confused with 運搬 (transport). Japanese distinguishes cleanly: 運搬 = moving materials; 動作 = worker body movements.

## 5. 「なぜ」を5回繰り返す — "Ask *why* five times"

Ohno's most famous teaching, taught at pp. 33–34 of the 1978 book. Widely reproduced across Japanese manufacturing teaching sites as a canonical teaching example.

### 原文 (the machine-breakdown dialogue, widely quoted in Japanese lean-teaching sites)

A cutting machine has stopped. Ohno walks a line worker through five rounds of *naze?* ("why?"):

1. なぜ機械が止まったのか？ → オーバーロードがかかってヒューズが切れたから。
2. なぜオーバーロードがかかったのか？ → 軸受部の潤滑が十分でなかったから。
3. なぜ十分に潤滑しなかったのか？ → 潤滑ポンプが十分に汲み上げていなかったから。
4. なぜ十分に汲み上げなかったのか？ → ポンプの軸が摩耗してガタガタになっていたから。
5. なぜ摩耗したのか？ → 濾過器（ろ過器）が付いておらず、切粉が入ったから。

### 英訳

1. Why did the machine stop? → An overload occurred and the fuse blew.
2. Why did the overload occur? → The bearing was not lubricated enough.
3. Why wasn't it lubricated enough? → The lubrication pump wasn't pumping up enough.
4. Why wasn't it pumping up enough? → The pump shaft was worn and loose.
5. Why was it worn? → There was no filter, so metal chips got in.

**Root cause / countermeasure:** install a filter on the lubrication pump. Not: replace the fuse.

### ニュアンス・ギャップ分析

- Ohno's own teaching in the book (paraphrase from pp. 33–34, widely reproduced on [monodukuri.com](https://www.monodukuri.com/gihou/article/821) and [monozukuri-japan.seesaa.net](https://monozukuri-japan.seesaa.net/article/503460730.html)): *if you stop short of five whys, you end up replacing the fuse or the pump shaft, and months later the same trouble recurs*.
- **The English "5 Whys" loses the *humility* of the Japanese.** なぜ is a soft, curious word. In practice it's taught as *自問自答* (*jimon-jitō*, "asking yourself and answering yourself") — the point is **introspection under a standard discipline**, not interrogation of a subordinate. Many English implementations turn 5 Whys into a blame cascade because they miss the *自問自答* framing.
- **The number 5 is not magical.** Ohno's point is that one or two whys rarely reach the real cause. Five is an *anchor to push past the premature answer*, not a target. English lean culture sometimes enforces "exactly 5." Japanese teaching says **「なぜの追及が足りないと…同じトラブルが再発する」** — ask until you stop hitting symptoms.
- **The countermeasure lives at the last why.** At #5 you install a filter. If you only went to #1 (fuse) or #3 (lubrication), you treat the symptom. The discipline is to keep going until you reach something you can change at the process/design level.
- **Note on the verb 汲み上げる (*kumiageru*, "to pump/draw up").** Some secondary sources render this as 組み上げる (assemble/build up) — that's an OCR or transcription error. The correct verb for pump action is 汲み上げる.

### 出典

- Ohno 1978, pp. 33–34
- [ものづくりドットコム — 大野耐一氏の「なぜなぜ五回」の真意](https://www.monodukuri.com/gihou/article/821)
- [monozukuri-japan — 元祖「なぜなぜ分析」](https://monozukuri-japan.seesaa.net/article/503460730.html)

## 6. 徹底的なムダの排除 — "thorough elimination of waste"

### 原文

Ohno's signature phrase for the *purpose* of TPS:

> **「徹底的なムダの排除」**
> (*tettei-teki na muda no haijo*)

### 英訳

Typically rendered as "thorough elimination of waste." More faithful: "elimination of waste, carried through to the very bottom."

### ニュアンス・ギャップ分析

- 徹底的 (*tettei-teki*) means *thoroughly, through-and-through, to the utmost*. The adverbial phrase 徹底して (*tettei shite*) means *all the way, without letting up*. The word contains 徹 (penetrate) + 底 (bottom) — *penetrate to the bottom*.
- English "thorough" is a mild intensifier. Japanese 徹底的 is almost religious. It implies *no half-measures, no exceptions, no tolerance*.
- The phrase 徹底的なムダの排除 is the *mission statement* of TPS in Japanese. Every kaizen activity, every 5-whys session, every standardized-work revision is supposed to serve this one objective. English adopters often miss this unifying purpose and treat TPS as a toolkit (kanban, SMED, poka-yoke, etc.) rather than as a mission.

### 出典

- Ohno 1978 (repeated throughout; it's the organizing principle of the book)
- [ダイヤモンド・オンライン — 大野耐一](https://diamond.jp/articles/-/7399)
- [Diamond 社 — book page](https://www.diamond.co.jp/book/9784478460016.html)

## 7. かんばん (Kanban)

### 原文

**かんばん** — ordinarily written in hiragana (not 看板, which would be "signboard"). The hiragana is deliberate: Ohno's *kanban* is a specific production-signal card, distinct from a street sign.

### ニュアンス

- The ordinary Japanese word 看板 (*kanban*, "signboard") is what everyone on the street sees. Ohno's かんばん is **a card on a box** — a signal that says "pull from upstream, replenish when consumed." The hiragana spelling is a small typographical signal that this is a *technical term*, not the generic word.
- English adopters write "kanban" in lowercase Roman, which is the closest English can get to the hiragana's visual-technical-term signal. But English readers often re-inflate it mentally to "sign" or "card," missing the specific *pull-replenishment* semantics.
- The concept's surface analogue, as Ohno documents, came from American supermarkets (スーパーマーケット方式): when a shelf empties, staff pull more from the back. The Japanese framing marries this to 自働化 and ジャストインタイム as the *means* to realize JIT inside a factory.

### 出典

- Ohno 1978, Chapter 2 and 3
- [Diamond 社 book page](https://www.diamond.co.jp/book/9784478460016.html) (summary identifies kanban as the means of realizing JIT + jidōka)

## Cross-cutting: why Ohno's Japanese is so hard to translate

1. **Kanji choice as argument.** 自働化 vs 自動化 is the clearest case. When Ohno chose 働 with the 亻 radical, the character itself is carrying the thesis. English has no way to do this; every translation of TPS loses it.
2. **Verb-based thinking.** Japanese verbs (挑戦する, 見極める, 果たす, 結集する, 徹底する) are the skeleton of Toyota's value statements. English turns them into abstract nouns (challenge, understanding, responsibility, teamwork, thoroughness), which neuters the action-orientation.
3. **Ordering as meaning.** ムリ → ムラ → ムダ encodes causation. つくりすぎのムダ first because it is the worst. English lists lose this.
4. **〜だけ (only/just) grammar.** Japanese particles can insist *and nothing more*. English adjectives ("right," "correct") cannot.
5. **Intensifier weight.** 徹底的 is a religious-strength word. "Thorough" is mild. Every TPS phrase that uses 徹底的 loses its pressure in English.
6. **Register.** Ohno's Japanese is plainspoken but intense — he was a shop-floor man. The English translation (Japan Management Association, 1988) reads more like a textbook. Ohno's own voice, still sharper, comes through most clearly in the posthumous collection 『トヨタ生産方式の原点』(2014).
