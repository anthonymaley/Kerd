## Chapter 1: Starting from Need

### The Oil Crisis Reveals Toyota's Advantage

The 1973 oil crisis exposed the fragility of mass-production thinking. While other companies suffered, Toyota sustained earnings. This drew attention to the Toyota Production System and forced the question: what was Toyota doing differently?

### Japan's Postwar Challenge

Japan faced a fundamental constraint after the war: low volume, high variety. The central question became:

> How to cut costs while producing small numbers of many types of cars.

Toyoda Kiichiro (1894-1952) set an audacious goal:

> Catch up with America in three years.

In 1937, Japanese worker productivity was roughly one-ninth of American productivity. Ohno refused to believe the gap was physical:

> Could an American really exert ten times more physical effort? Surely, Japanese people were wasting something.

This observation -- that the gap was waste, not effort -- became the seed of TPS.

### Two Pillars of TPS

The Toyota Production System rests on two pillars:

1. **Just-in-Time (JIT)**: In a flow process, the right parts needed in assembly reach the assembly line at the time they are needed and only in the amount needed.

   > In a flow process, the right parts needed in assembly reach the assembly line at the time they are needed and only in the amount needed.

2. **Autonomation (jidoka)**: Automation with a human touch. The machine stops automatically when an abnormality is detected. Originated from Toyoda Sakichi's auto-activated loom.

   > One worker can attend several machines.

Ohno uses a baseball analogy: autonomation is the individual skill of each player; JIT is the teamwork that coordinates the whole team.

### Cost Reduction Is the Goal

Efficiency is not pursued for its own sake. The goal is cost reduction. Ohno reframes the cost principle:

The conventional formula -- "selling price = profit + actual cost" -- is backwards. The market sets the price. Profit comes only from reducing cost.

### Establishing Flow (1947)

At the Koromo plant in 1947, Ohno rearranged machines in processing sequence to establish production flow. Workers resisted the shift to "one operator, many machines." Ohno was patient and did not press for drastic changes.

### Production Leveling

Leveling means spreading production evenly. 1,000 parts per month across 25 working days = 40 per day = 1 every 12 minutes. Ohno warned against "Dekansho production" -- cramming everything at the end of the month -- which nearly killed them.

### Necessity and Consciousness

> Necessity is the mother of invention.

Improvements must be based on need, not theory. Ohno calls for a revolution in consciousness:

> A Revolution in Consciousness Is Indispensable.

Overproduction waste comes from an agricultural, hoarding mentality. Industrial society requires different thinking:

> Industrial society must develop the courage, or rather the common sense, to procure only what is needed when it is needed and in the amount needed.

## Chapter 2: Evolution of the Toyota Production System

### The Five Whys

The 5 Whys method is TPS's scientific backbone. Ohno's canonical example:

1. Why did the machine stop? -- A fuse blew from an overload.
2. Why was there an overload? -- The bearing was not sufficiently lubricated.
3. Why was it not lubricated sufficiently? -- The lubrication pump was not pumping sufficiently.
4. Why was it not pumping sufficiently? -- The shaft of the pump was worn and rattling.
5. Why was the shaft worn out? -- There was no strainer attached and metal scrap got in.

> By asking why five times and answering it each time, we can get to the real cause of the problem.

> The Toyota production system has been built on the practice and evolution of this scientific approach.

### Complete Analysis of Waste

Ohno's foundational equation:

> Present capacity = work + waste.

True efficiency means zero waste, 100% work. The seven wastes are:

1. Overproduction
2. Time on hand (waiting)
3. Transportation
4. Processing itself (over-processing)
5. Stock on hand (inventory)
6. Movement
7. Making defective products

> Improving efficiency makes sense only when it is tied to cost reduction.

### Plant-First Principle

> The production plant is manufacturing's major source of information.

Go to the plant. Observe. The answers are there.

### Standard Work Sheet

Three elements define standard work:

1. **Cycle time** -- the time allowed to make one piece or unit
2. **Work sequence** -- the order of operations a worker performs
3. **Standard inventory** -- the minimum in-process inventory needed to keep the process running

> I have always said that it should take only three days to train new workers in proper work procedures.

Training is not passive:

> The trainer must actually take the hands of the workers and teach them.

### Teamwork and Baton Passing

Ohno uses a boat race with eight rowers:

> The best way to propel the boat faster is for everyone to distribute force equally.

Manufacturing is teamwork, not individual output. He extends this with the track relay concept of baton passing:

> In a manufacturing job done by four or five people, the parts should be handed over as if they were batons.

Workers should help set up machines for delayed workers, then return to their positions. The smooth handoff matters more than individual speed.

### The Supermarket Idea (1953)

The later process acts as a customer; the earlier process acts as a store.

> From the supermarket we got the idea of viewing the earlier process in a production line as a kind of store.

The customer (later process) goes to the store (earlier process) to get what is needed, when it is needed, in the amount needed. The store restocks only what has been taken.

### Kanban

A kanban is a piece of paper in a vinyl envelope. Three categories of information: pickup, transfer, and production.

> One piece of paper provided at a glance the following information: production quantity, time, method, sequence...

**Six rules of kanban:**

1. The later process goes to the earlier process to pick up products.
2. The earlier process produces only the amount withdrawn.
3. No pickup or production without a kanban.
4. A kanban must be attached to the goods.
5. 100% defect-free -- do not send defective products to the next process.
6. Reduce the number of kanban over time.

> Incorrect Use Causes Problems.

Kanban used improperly makes things worse, not better.

### Flow Before Kanban

Establishing flow is the prerequisite. Kanban cannot be bolted onto a broken process.

> Unless one completely grasps this method of doing work so that things will flow, it is impossible to go right into the kanban system.

### Ten Years to Company-Wide Adoption

It took from 1953 to 1962 to establish kanban across Toyota.

> Although it sounds like a long time, I think it was natural because we were breaking in totally new concepts.

In 1962, kanban was adopted company-wide. Cooperating firms learned by visiting Toyota and watching directly:

> They would have had difficulty understanding the system without seeing it in action.

### Use Your Authority to Encourage Them

Ohno experimented within his own sphere of authority, expanding the system as his responsibilities grew. He did not wait for top-down permission.

### Production Leveling in Practice

> Mountains Should Be Low and Valleys Should Be Shallow.

Corona and Carina models flowed alternately on the same assembly line. For 10,000 Coronas per month: 250 sedans + 125 hardtops + 125 wagons daily, sequenced as sedan, hardtop, sedan, wagon -- never in batches.

### Setup Time Reduction

- 1940s: 2-3 hours
- 1950s: under 1 hour, then down to 15 minutes
- Late 1960s: 3 minutes

This progression made small lot sizes economically viable.

### Carrying Carts as Kanban

Limited-capacity carrying carts serve as physical kanban. When the cart is full, production stops. No paper kanban needed -- the cart itself prevents overproduction.

### Elastic Nature of Kanban

Even with irregular usage patterns (e.g., propeller shaft balance weights in 5 types), kanban worked by maintaining a constant inventory of each type. The system absorbs variability.

## Chapter 3: Further Development

### The Autonomic Nervous System of Business

> An autonomic nerve means making judgments autonomously at the lowest possible level.

Workers on the floor decide: when to stop production, what sequence to follow, when overtime is needed -- without consulting production control. The organization responds like a body, not a bureaucracy.

### Plans Are Like the Spine

> The sturdier the human spine, the more easily it bends.

Sticking rigidly to a plan once set is like putting the body in a cast. Business needs reflexes:

> Response instantly and smoothly to small changes in the plan without having to go to the brain.

### Provide Necessary Information When Needed

Computers generate excess information. Ohno warns:

> Too much information throws the production field into confusion.

Toyota uses computers for planning and leveling, but does not push information to every process. The Toyota-style information system flows: annual plan to monthly schedule to daily level. The daily sequence schedule goes ONLY to the final assembly line. All other processes receive their instructions from kanban.

### Fine Adjustment

Kanban allows automatic adjustment within limits. The ratios of car types can shift without anyone announcing changes -- the system self-corrects through pull signals.

### What Is True Economy?

Manpower reduction is the means to cost reduction. But economy requires judgment:

> A $500 device to save one worker is waste if changing the work sequence achieves the same result at no cost.

Always choose the simplest, cheapest option. Judging between A and B, and selecting the best among A, B, and C options -- this is the discipline of true economy.

### Re-Examining Waste

> Too many workers, equipment, and product only increase the cost and cause secondary waste.

Waste generates waste in a vicious cycle. Excess capacity in workers leads to excess production, which leads to excess inventory, which requires excess handling, storage, and management.

### Generate Excess Capacity

When there is surplus capacity, in-house production costs only the variable expense. Reduce lot sizes when there is excess.

> The most important thing is to know the extent of excess capacity at all times.

### Understanding

> I believe it has a specific meaning -- to approach an objective positively and comprehend its nature.

Three categories of worker movement:

1. **Waste** -- eliminate immediately
2. **Non-value-added work** -- change conditions to eliminate
3. **Value-added work** -- processing that changes shape or character of the product

The critical distinction:

> Moving is not necessarily working. Working means actually advancing the process.

### Full Work System

Standard inventory is maintained automatically. The earlier process starts when later-process inventory drops, and stops when it reaches the required number. No external signal needed beyond the inventory level itself.

### Required Numbers Are All-Important

> Production quantity equals market demand or actual orders.

There are two ways to increase efficiency: increase output or reduce workers. But when demand is unchanged:

> Increasing efficiency by increasing production while demand is unchanged = "apparent increase of efficiency" -- something we must never attempt.

### Tortoise and Hare

> The slower but consistent tortoise causes less waste and is much more desirable than the speedy hare who races ahead and then stops occasionally to doze.

Steady, level production beats bursts of speed followed by idle time.

### Take Good Care of Old Equipment

> A machine's value is not determined by its years of service or its age. It is determined by the earning power it still retains.

Depreciation and book value are artificial terms for accounting purposes:

> If adequate maintenance has been done, replacement with a new machine is never cheaper.

### 0.1 Worker Is Still One Worker

Ohno distinguishes between "labor saving," "worker saving," and "reducing the number of workers." Saving 0.9 of a person means nothing in practice -- at least one full person must be saved.

Work improvement should contribute half or one-third of total cost reduction BEFORE equipment improvement is considered.

### Toyota's 1950 Labor Dispute

Toyota had a labor dispute in 1950, followed immediately by Korean War demands.

> We met these demands with just enough people and still increased production.

Toyota produced the same quantity with 20-30% fewer workers.

### Do Not Make Isolated Islands

> Do not make isolated islands.

Even if there is only enough work for one person, group 5-6 workers together as a team. Isolation kills teamwork and hides waste.

### Management by Ninjutsu

> Management should be done not by arithmetic but by ninjutsu.

The Corolla story: 80 workers were making 5,000 engines. The boss said 10,000 units would need 160 workers. Ohno's response:

> In grade school I was taught that two times eight equals sixteen. After all these years, do you think I should learn that from you?

100 workers ended up making over 10,000 units. Linear scaling is lazy thinking.

### In an Art Form, Action Is Required

The Japanese character for "art" combines "require" and "action." Technology talk without technology practice is empty. Implementation matters.

### Profit-Making Industrial Engineering

Toyota-style IE = MIE (mokeru IE, profit-making IE). IE must result in cost reductions and profit increases, not just studies and reports.

### Surviving in Slow Growth

> A total management system is needed.

Individual tools are not enough. The system must work as a whole.

## Chapter 4: Genealogy of the Toyota Production System

### Toyoda Sakichi: The Power of Observation

Sakichi spent an entire day watching his grandmother's loom. From this came his philosophy:

> Find a subject to think about, stare at an object until a hole is almost bored through it, and find out its essential nature.

Ohno echoes this:

> Stand on the production floor all day and watch -- you will eventually discover what has to be done. I cannot emphasize this too much.

### The Auto-Activated Loom

Toyoda Sakichi's auto-activated loom applied human intelligence to machines. It took 25 years from first conception (1901) to completion, accomplished entirely by Japanese people.

### Toyoda Kiichiro: Adapting, Not Copying

Kiichiro articulated the founding principle in 1933:

> We shall learn production techniques from the American method of mass production. But we will not copy it as is. We shall use our own research and creativity to develop a production method that suits our own country's situation.

This is the origin of the JIT idea.

### Provide Good Equipment Even If the Factory Is Simple

The Toyoda approach: use barracks for factory buildings, but spend the best money on machines.

> If we are not prepared to spend money for good machines, we should not be in automobile manufacturing.

### Making Products That Have Value

The government protected the industry with the auto manufacturing law, but Toyoda Kiichiro warned against relying on protection:

> Good marketing and skillful advertising might allow us to deceive the buyers for a while -- but not for long.

### The Chessplayer's View

Both Toyoda Sakichi and Kiichiro had the overview perspective of a chessplayer.

> They knew how to checkmate.

Sakichi's thinking was entirely self-generated:

> He would not read catalogues or books... His thinking and inventing were accomplished completely by himself.

### In Search of Something Japanese

Toyoda Sakichi sold his auto-activated loom patent to Platt Brothers (UK) for one million yen. That money funded automobile research. The search for Japanese originality -- adapting, not copying -- flows directly into TPS.

## Chapter 5: The True Intention of the Ford System

### Ford and Toyota: Both Based on Flow

The Ford system is mass production based on work flow. The Toyota system is also based on work flow. The critical difference:

> While Sorensen worried about warehousing parts, Toyota eliminated the warehouse.

### Small Lot Sizes and Quick Setup

Ford meant large lots and planned mass production. Toyota means the opposite:

> Our production slogan is "small lot sizes and quick setups."

### Ford's Foresight

Ohno believed Ford was a natural rationalist:

> If the American king of cars were still alive, he would be headed in the same direction as Toyota.

### Ford on Waste

From Ford's own book *Today and Tomorrow* (1926):

> My theory of waste goes back of the thing itself into the labour of producing it.

Material costs mean nothing until management handles them properly.

> The ideal is to have nothing to salvage.

### Standards from Ford

> One has to go rather slowly on fixing standards... There is the standardizing which marks inertia, and the standardizing which marks progress.

Standards should come from inside manufacturing, not imposed from outside. The purpose of a standard is to be improved upon.

### The True End of Industry

> The true end of industry is to liberate mind and body from the drudgery of existence by filling the world with well-made, low-priced products.

### Ford's Successors Misinterpreted

Ford's successors kept large lot sizes in upstream processes. They missed the point:

> They ended up with the concept "the larger the lot size, the better." This builds a dam, so to speak, and stops the flow.

### Prevention Is Better than Healing

> Toyota's strength does not come from its healing processes -- it comes from preventive maintenance.

### Efficiency Is Not Quantity and Speed

Ford asked "Are we moving too fast?" Toyota suppresses overproduction:

> At the heart of this is our intention to reform the existing and deeply rooted concept of "faster and more."

## Chapter 6: Surviving the Low-Growth Period

### High-Growth Masked Waste

During the high-growth period:

> Whatever was made was sold.

This masked the waste embedded in mass-production machinery and thinking.

### Automation Without Autonomation

Machines could not judge or stop by themselves:

> The number of workers did not decrease with automation. Manual work just changed names.

### The Oil Crisis Forced the Issue

The oil crisis forced Toyota to reduce production for the first time since the 1930s. It exposed a critical problem: autonomated machines still needed a fixed number of operators even at 50% production.

### Reducing the Number of Workers

The solution: flexible production lines where work can be done by any number of workers regardless of production quantities. Multi-skilled worker training must happen while times are normal -- not during the crisis.

### The Deciding Factor

> How many can attain [productivity increases] during the more difficult circumstances induced by low growth? This is the deciding factor in the success or failure of an enterprise.

Ohno makes the challenge even sharper:

> There must be hundreds of people around the world who can improve productivity and efficiency by increasing production quantity... But few people in the world can raise productivity when production quantities decrease.

### Specific Countermeasures

> For every problem, we must have a specific countermeasure. A vague statement that waste should be eliminated, or that there are too many workers, will not convince anybody.

### Reverse Common Sense

> I urge all managers... to be more flexible in their thinking.

Inverse thinking -- challenging established assumptions -- is required to survive low-growth periods.

## Glossary (Key Definitions)

### Andon
Line-stop indicator board using green, yellow, and red lights to signal normal operation, a call for help, or a line stop respectively. Makes the status of the production line visible at a glance.

### Autonomation (Jidoka)
> Transferring human intelligence to a machine.

A machine is given the ability to detect abnormalities and stop itself. Distinct from simple automation.

### Baka-Yoke (Mistake-Proofing)
Six examples of devices designed to prevent defects. Physical mechanisms that make it impossible to perform an operation incorrectly.

### Baton Passing Zone
The track relay method applied to the production line. Workers overlap at transitions, handing work off smoothly like runners passing a baton.

### Do Not Make Isolated Islands
Group workers together for teamwork. Isolated workers hide waste and break flow.

### Five Why's
> 5W = 1H (Five Why's equal One How).

Ask why five times to find the root cause. The answer to the fifth why becomes the how -- the countermeasure.

### Just-in-Time (JIT)
Producing and delivering the right items at the right time in the right amounts. One of the two main pillars of TPS, alongside autonomation.

### Kanban
Types of kanban:
- **Withdrawal kanban** -- authorizes the later process to pick up parts
- **Transport kanban** -- authorizes movement between processes
- **Signal kanban** -- triggers production of a batch (used for stamping, etc.)
- **In-process / production ordering kanban** -- authorizes production at a process

### Labor Saving, Worker Saving, Reducing Number of Workers
A progression of increasing impact:
- **Labor saving** -- reducing effort (may save 0.1 or 0.5 of a worker)
- **Worker saving** -- reorganizing so one fewer worker is needed
- **Reducing the number of workers** -- actually removing the person from the line

Only the last two produce real cost reduction.

### Moving vs Working
> Progress = work. Movement does not equal work.

A worker may be moving constantly but adding no value. Working means advancing the process -- changing the shape, form, or character of the product.

### Multi-Process Operation System
One operator operating multiple different processes (e.g., lathe, mill, drill). Distinct from multi-unit operation, where one operator runs several machines of the same type.

### Operating Rate vs Operable Rate
- **Operating rate** -- can and should vary with demand. Running at 100% when demand is low means overproduction.
- **Operable rate** -- should always be 100%. The machine must be capable of running whenever needed.

### Production Leveling (Heijunka)
Never assemble the same model in a batch at final assembly. Mix models in sequence to smooth demand on upstream processes.

### Profit-Making IE
Toyota-style industrial engineering tied directly to cost reduction. Theory without profit impact is not IE.

### Real Cause
> Underneath the "cause" of a problem, the real cause is hidden. We must dig up the real cause by asking why, why, why, why, why.

### Required Numbers = Production Quantity
Produce only what the market requires. No more.

### Small Lot Sizes and Quick Setups
Setup time progression:
- 1940s: 2-3 hours
- 1950s: 1 hour to 15 minutes
- Present (late 1970s): 3 minutes

Small lots become economical only when setup times are driven down.

### Standard Work Procedures
Three elements:
1. **Cycle time** -- derived from required output and available time
2. **Work sequence** -- the order of operations
3. **Standard inventory** -- minimum in-process stock to maintain flow

### Stopping the Line
> A production line that does not stop is either a perfect line or a line with big problems.

If the line never stops, it means problems are being passed downstream rather than surfaced.

### Visual Control
Autonomation clarifies normal vs abnormal conditions. When a machine stops itself, the abnormality becomes visible immediately. No one needs to watch -- the system announces its own problems.

### Seven Wastes (Muda)
1. Overproduction
2. Waiting
3. Transporting
4. Over-processing
5. Inventories
6. Moving (unnecessary motion)
7. Making defective parts

Overproduction is the worst because it generates all the others.

### Work Flow vs Work Forced to Flow
A conveyor carrying goods is not work flow. Value being added while the product flows -- that is work flow. The distinction is whether transformation is happening, not whether movement is happening.

### Work Improvement vs Equipment Improvement
Always pursue work improvement first. Equipment improvement takes money and cannot be undone. Work improvement -- changing sequences, eliminating waste in motion, rearranging tasks -- costs nothing and can be reversed if it does not work.
