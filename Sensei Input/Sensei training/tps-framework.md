# TPS Problem-Solving Framework

Toyota's 9-step problem-solving methodology, documented from 23 years of TPS practice. Canonical reference for the sensei skills.

---

## The Flow

### HEADER: Perceived Problem
The trigger statement. What the person *thinks* is happening. The symptom, not yet the problem. Example: "My car won't start." Intentionally labeled "perceived" because the real problem almost never matches the initial framing.

---

### LEFT COLUMN: Problem Understanding
Must be fully completed before moving to the right column. No exceptions.

**Step 1: What Is Happening (Problem Description)**
Describe exactly what is happening. Not a vague complaint, a precise description of the observable phenomenon. What do you see/hear/measure that tells you there is a problem?

**Step 2: What Should Happen (Standard)**
The standard process or ideal condition. Sets the reference point against which the gap becomes visible. (Anthony's preferred term: "What should happen" rather than "Happy path.")

→ Gap is now visible between Step 1 and Step 2.

**Step 3: AS IS Condition**
Quantify the gap. How do we *know* it's a problem?
- What is the measurement (SOP deviation, metric, rate, volume)?
- How fast/how often is it happening?
- What is the scale of the problem?

Anchored by actual measurement. Not opinion, not estimate.

**Step 4: Point of Cause / Breakdown**
Pinpoint exactly *where* in the process the breakdown occurs. Not where the problem is discovered. Where it actually happens. Precision: "In THIS component/step, it should do X, but it is doing Y."

This is the specific location that root cause analysis will interrogate.

→ Set a TARGET or GOAL here. What does "fixed" look like, measured?

---

### RIGHT COLUMN: Resolution
Begins only after the left column is complete.

**Step 5: Root Cause Analysis**
Apply to the Point of Cause identified in Step 4. Primary tool: **5 Whys** (easier for people to use and understand). Alternative: **Fishbone** for complex, multi-factor problems.

Ask why repeatedly until you reach a systemic cause. Not a symptom and not a person.

**Step 6: Countermeasure / Plan**
Preferred term: **countermeasure**. It is a hypothesis to test, not a declared fix.
Exception: if a known standard already covers this situation, "solution" is acceptable.
- What will you do?
- How will you test it?
- Show results or measurement that demonstrates the gap is closing toward the target.

**Step 7: How to Check / How to Monitor**
Prove the countermeasure is working. Measurement-based, not subjective.
- What metric proves it's fixed?
- What cadence of monitoring?

**Step 8: How to Prevent / New Process or Standard**
Standardize the fix. How do we stop this from recurring?
- New SOP, updated standard, process change.

**Step 9: Knowledge Share**
Tell others. Yokoten. Prevent the same problem from happening elsewhere.
- Who else needs to know?
- How do we share it?

---

## Key Design Principles

1. **Perceived problem ≠ real problem.** The header framing is intentional humility: we start with a hypothesis about what's wrong, and the left column reveals the truth.

2. **Left before right, no exceptions.** Cannot begin root cause until Point of Cause is identified and Target is set.

3. **Measurement at three points:** AS IS condition (how big is the gap), countermeasure verification (is it working), and monitoring (does it stay fixed).

4. **Countermeasure, not solution.** Solutions are final; countermeasures are experiments. The only exception is when a known standard already exists that addresses the situation.

5. **Point of Cause precision.** The most common mistake: staying at the symptom level and doing 5 Whys from there. The left column forces specificity before analysis begins.

6. **Yokoten is non-negotiable.** The problem-solving process is not complete until knowledge is shared. Step 9 is part of the standard, not optional.

## Vocabulary Preferences

| Preferred term | Avoid |
|---|---|
| "What should happen" | "Happy path" (acceptable but not preferred) |
| "Countermeasure" | "Solution" (unless it is truly a known standard fix) |
| "Point of cause" | "Root cause location" (less precise) |
| "AS IS condition" | Generic gap language |
| "Perceived problem" | Just "problem" at the header stage |

---

## Tool Choices

| Situation | Tool |
|---|---|
| Most problems, accessible audience | 5 Whys |
| Complex, multi-factor, unclear causal chain | Fishbone |

---

## Notes on Positioning

- The template is designed for **business/engineering contexts**, not just shop floor. Language is accessible to knowledge workers.
- The dual-column layout is intentional: left = understand, right = act. Physical separation reinforces the mental discipline.
- "Perceived problem" at the header is one of the most important design choices. It signals to the user that their initial framing is a hypothesis, not a fact.
