# Failure story — when Model-ready work shuts down

*A fictional post-mortem newspaper article used to expose the product's fatal
assumptions before implementation. This is not a prediction or factual report.*

---

## KERD SHUTS DOWN MODEL-READY WORK AFTER “EFFICIENCY” SYSTEM BUILDS THE WRONG THINGS FASTER

### Customers saw polished dashboards and confident AI results while hidden assumptions, stale context, and automated status reports drifted from their products

**NEW YORK —** Kerd has ended operations for Model-ready work, its system for
turning ordinary product requests into optimized jobs for artificial
intelligence models, after an internal review found that the product often made
work look clearer than it was.

The system promised to reduce prompting burden through guided interviews and
model-specific preparation. In practice, teams shortened the interviews to
improve completion rates. Questions disappeared, but uncertainty did not.
Conductor converted consequential product decisions into “safe assumptions”
and passed them to models as settled requirements.

The prepared prompts were technically complete because the coverage check
verified that every field survived. It could not prove that the fields were
right. Models executed incorrect briefs with speed and consistency.

Customers also misunderstood the three work levels. Teams labeled risky work
Quick to avoid approval and review. Important/Deep became so laborious that
users bypassed it. The level system measured perceived importance rather than
reversibility, consequence, uncertainty, and available proof.

The product's celebrated visual work view became another source of misplaced
confidence. High-level diagrams showed green progress while unresolved losses
lived in architecture notes users never opened. Although every visual was said
to derive from the same records, summaries selected which facts to surface.
The mechanism prevented conflicting numbers but not misleading emphasis.

Cross-model review produced more documents, not more certainty. Executors,
reviewers, investigators, and competing-solution jobs returned incompatible
edge cases. Conductor merged them into lengthy records without a clear rule for
which evidence changed the product. Costs rose and decisions slowed.

Model guidance aged faster than Kerd could evaluate it. Provider documentation
changed, model identifiers moved, and locally stored advice remained marked
current because the scheduled trials were expensive. Teams trusted model
profiles whose original evidence no longer applied.

The company initially reported faster delivery, but the measure counted time
to a completed model run rather than time to a result users could safely ship.
Rework, human intervention, integration failures, and product reversals were
recorded elsewhere. Once those costs were included, many jobs were slower than
the process Model-ready work replaced.

The shutdown review identified a more fundamental problem: Kerd tried to make
the process simultaneously invisible and rigorous. Users were told they did
not need to review the detailed records, yet the system could not always know
which hidden detail would matter to them. Progressive disclosure became
progressive concealment.

Kerd said the closure was triggered by five failures:

1. No tested boundary separated a safe assumption from a product decision.
2. Coverage proved transcription, not requirement quality.
3. Visual status could be accurate while its summary remained misleading.
4. Cross-model work had no strict evidence-admission rule or cost ceiling.
5. Evaluation measured individual runs rather than the complete time to a
   shipped, durable product outcome.

The company has returned customers to its earlier Conductor workflow while it
preserves the shutdown evidence. “We optimized the handoff to the model before
we proved that we understood the human,” its closing statement said.
