The seats, in detail.

The same nine seats as the hall on the front page. For each one: what goes wrong without it, what it does in plain words, and the skill that plays it.

Two words run through all of them. Rehearsal is the part you are in: you and the AI work the piece through turn by turn, in any order, shipping as you go, while what gets settled goes in a sketchbook. The concert is what happens once you say you are ready: the work is written down as a score and a team of agents performs it. There are no turn-by-turn questions in the concert, because the asking already happened.

In the hall. Best seat in the house.

You.

it’s your piece

Without you
There is no piece, only a chat that stops when it feels finished.
With you
You decide what done means and when rehearsal ends. Nothing is performed that you have not agreed.

You bring the piece of work and a rough idea of done. In rehearsal the AI does the work and you react, and that is how you both find out what you meant. The conductor may say it thinks you are ready, or name the one or two things still missing; if you call it ready while something is open, you are told what, and going ahead anyway is your call, written into the sketchbook. Choosing a piece of work approves none of what follows: builds, installs, pushes and anything that costs money each get their own yes, and the finished concert reaches main only on your go.

Played by you. Read the getting started guide

Centre front, on the podium.

Conductor.

/kerd:conductor

Without it
Done is whatever the AI says.
With it
Done is pinned down in rehearsal before the concert, and before the concert ends the result is compared with your goals. A gap the score cannot answer comes back to you for that one point, not to the start.

For a substantial build, design or piece of workflow, new or already underway. In rehearsal it guides, interviews and prompts until, between you, you hold what a performance needs: the idea and why it matters, the goals, the constraints and the design. What gets settled goes in the sketchbook, one per piece of work, so “where are we?” is answered from there and not from whatever the chat happens to remember. In the concert it staffs and sends out the players, checks every return against the score rather than taking it on trust, and performs on a branch of its own, so main never holds half-built work. A small fix or a status question stays direct; it will not drag either through an intake.

Played by Conductor. Read the Conductor guide

In the wings, where the running order lives.

Stage manager.

/kerd:switch

Without it
Every new chat starts from nothing, and you re-explain.
With it
You sit down and it tells you where things stand, what is open, and the one thing it would do next and why. On this machine or another.

Switch keeps your place between sittings. Switch In reads the project and answers on one screen, in plain English: where things stand, what changed last time, the open work one line each, and one recommendation with the reason it comes first. Then it asks a single question, “Start a Conductor session?”, and waits. Switch Out reads what actually changed, adds what the sitting settled to the sketchbook, and saves the next step with its reason. Two more moves cover the rest: To carries a mid-work position to another machine through GitHub, and Roll carries a long build into a fresh session when its context fills, without starting over.

Played by Switch. Read the Switch guide

At the easel, upstage.

Illustrator.

/kerd:visuals

Without it
You agree to words you can’t picture.
With it
A proposal with connected parts, a branch or a before and after arrives with a picture, saved and rendered. Nobody asks whether you would like a diagram.

Visuals draws the thing you are discussing: how the parts connect, who owns what, where the boundary of the work sits, what changes between before and after. It draws at product level first, people, capabilities, work and outcomes, and hands back a saved, rendered picture you can open, never a diagram made of text boxes in a code fence. What is proposed, partial or unknown is labelled as such, rather than quietly filled in with a guess. You can also ask for one directly, when you want to see the shape of a plan before you agree to it.

Played by Visuals. Read the Visuals guide

At the desk at the back. Finishes before anyone plays.

Composer.

drafts the score

Without it
The AI builds before anyone knows what done is.
With it
When you say you are ready, what rehearsal settled is written as a score: the plan, one step per part. The asking is over before the playing starts.

The score is written as you go, not in one sitting at the end. Where the steps are already clear, the conductor writes them itself. Where a passage needs design or reasoning it cannot settle with confidence, it calls in a composer to write that passage and hand it back. The composer writes and never sends anyone out. Each step names what it should produce, the ground it covers, what it depends on, what it owns and what counts as evidence, so a player can perform it without stopping to ask. A step that turns out to be wrong goes back to whoever wrote it and is repaired, not reworded until a check passes.

Played by Conductor, which calls the composer in. Read the Conductor guide

In the arcs, side by side.

Players.

agents, side by side

Without them
One chat plays every part, one at a time.
With them
Several agents at once, each given one step and running alongside the others, and every return checked before it counts.

In the concert every independent part of the score goes to its own player, as many at once as the score allows, each at a model and effort fitted to its part. When a batch comes back, the conductor checks each player’s claim against its part of the score and reads the actual changes against how things stood before it sent them. The results go into the sketchbook. When the context window runs low, the work rolls into a fresh session at the end of a batch and carries on under the same agreement. If a player meets something the score cannot answer, that part stops and comes back to you for that one point.

Played by Conductor, which sends the score out to them. Read the Conductor guide

In the box. A clear view, and no stake in the build.

Second opinion.

/kerd:agent, Codex or Claude

Without it
The AI marks its own homework.
With it
Another Claude or Codex session reads the work, given the brief and the sources, not the builder’s account of what it did. A model that has not spent the last hour convincing itself.

Agent gets a contribution from another AI session and brings the answer back: Claude asking Codex, Codex asking Claude, or either one asking a session that already has the context you need. It keeps track of which session is which, so “ask Codex to review this” reaches the partner you paired with, not a stranger who has never seen the work. A partner can hold a standing role and a review rhythm, at checkpoints, before a push, at the end or on request, and the conductor plans its reviews from that. A partner can read but not approve: a review is not permission to edit, and commits, pushes and releases stay yours.

Played by Agent. Read the Agent guide

Backstage, with the tuning fork.

Tuner.

/kerd:tend, /kerd:slainte

Without it
The project drifts out of tune.
With it
Tend checks the repo’s structure against current convention. Slainte reports where a doc’s claims no longer match the code, with the evidence that found each one.

Two halves of project health, checked separately. Tend keeps a repo’s structure and plumbing in shape: directories, config files, naming, stray files, the hooks. Reach for it on a new repo, or after a Kerd update. Slainte keeps the content honest: whether a doc’s claims still match the code, whether a count is stale, whether the release notes describe what actually shipped. It runs on demand for one area, or as a release pass when you invoke it at a release, and every finding carries the command or comparison that found it. Tend never commits its own changes, and Slainte’s on-demand audits only report.

Played by Tend and Slainte. Read the Tend and Slainte guide

Backstage, by the shelves.

Librarian.

/kerd:kivna

Without it
What you learn leaves with the chat.
With it
What you learn goes into an Obsidian vault in plain words, updated in place rather than piled up in dated logs.

Kivna keeps what you learn about a project in an Obsidian vault a person can read. Reach for it at a natural break: after finishing a task, before the context gets long, or when something important gets decided. Saving is deliberate, so the vault is exactly as fresh as its last save. It writes what was learned in plain words, never a mirror of the repo’s files, and it overwrites pages as the project moves instead of adding to a growing log.

Played by Kivna, with Skriv beside it. Read the Kivna and Skriv guide

Skriv keeps the librarian company. It has no seat of its own. It keeps your writing sounding like a person wrote it, checking prose against a kill list of words no one actually says and the habits that give an AI away, and reporting each one by line. It rewrites only when you ask it to fix, and it touches prose only, never code or commit messages. /kerd:skriv

That is the hall. To take your seat, it is two commands in Claude Code: install Kerd.
